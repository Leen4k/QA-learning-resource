The one-page test plan

Seven questions to answer, and the rules for how little you can write in answering them.

The rule

The seven areas are a checklist for your thinking, not a table of contents for your document.
Answer all seven. Compress freely. Never delete the thinking.

Source of the seven: ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §5.1.1. Source of the compression rule: Michael Bolton, What Should A Test Plan Contain? — write “the very least expensive representation of the idea that can sufficiently store or communicate the idea.”

Decide the size before you write

Tool Product
For You and your team An auditor, client, regulator
Job Help you accomplish the mission Supply someone else what they want
Length One page, or less As long as the audience requires
Failure Too polished to be worth the time Too terse to be accepted

Kaner’s question, via Bolton. Ask it first; it settles most arguments about length before they start.

The template

Block Answer this Budget
Scope
context of testing		 What am I testing, against what test basis, and what is explicitly out? 2–3 lines
Assumptions & constraints What must be true for this plan to hold? What limits me? 2–3 lines
Risks, ranked
risk register		 Top five. Each with impact and likelihood scored separately. 5 lines
Approach Which levels, types and techniques, in what order, and why that order? 4–6 lines
Entry What must exist before I can start? 1–2 lines
Exit What must be true before I say it is done? 1–2 lines
Schedule & people
budget, schedule, stakeholders, communication 		By when, by whom, signed off by whom, reported where? 1 line

Three of the seven syllabus areas — stakeholders, communication, and budget and schedule — collapse into that last line on a normal internal feature. That is compression, not omission.

Entry and exit criteria — patterns that work

Entry (Definition of Ready) Exit (Definition of Done)
Test data and accounts exist All planned tests executed
Environment deployed; quick checks passing No unresolved defect remains for a top-ranked risk
Acceptance criteria written and reviewed Top N risks each covered by an executed test
Tools and access available Unfinished work listed with residual risk, accepted in writing

Adapted from ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §5.1.3, which splits exit criteria into measures of thoroughness (coverage, unresolved defects, defect density, failed detailed checks) and binary yes/no criteria (planned tests executed, static testing performed, all defects reported, regression tests automated).

Three traps

Tempting Why it fails Instead
“Exit when every test passes” Treats a cosmetic bug as equal to a release-threatening failure Block only on defects tied to release risk
Inlining every detailed test Unreadable on day one, stale on day two Link to the supporting tests from the approach block
A day-by-day schedule False precision — §5.1.4: estimates are “always subject to estimation error” A range, and a named re-plan point

The permitted escape hatch

§5.1.3: “Running out of time or budget can also be viewed as valid exit criteria. Even without other exit criteria being satisfied, it can be acceptable to end testing under such circumstances, if the stakeholders have reviewed and accepted the risk to go live without further testing.”

Stopping because you ran out of time is legitimate. Stopping quietly is not. The written acceptance is what turns a failure into a decision.
