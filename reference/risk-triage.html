Quality risk triage

One page for deciding what to test first, and for defending the decision.

The formula

Risk level = likelihood × impact
Likelihood = how probable is this failure?
Impact = how much harm if it happens?

Scored Low = 1, Medium = 2, High = 3, risk levels run 1–9. The number is a sorting aid, not a truth. Its real job is to force the two factors apart so each gets its own reason.

Likelihood Low Likelihood Med Likelihood High
Impact High 3 — test 6 — test early 9 — test first
Impact Med 2 — if time 4 — test 6 — test early
Impact Low 1 — drop it 2 — if time 3 — test

Evidence for each dial

A score without a reason is a guess. Reach for these when asked “why?”

Raises likelihood

  • New or heavily changed code
  • Component with past defect history (defects cluster — principle 4)
  • High complexity, many branches
  • Unfamiliar technology or a new team
  • Behaviour that differs between staging and production
  • Integrations and third-party dependencies
  • Rushed or ambiguous requirements
  • Anything invisible on the happy path

Raises impact

  • Number and type of users affected
  • Money moved, lost or mis-stated
  • Data loss or corruption
  • Legal, regulatory, privacy or safety exposure
  • Reputational or trust damage
  • No workaround available to the user
  • Failure is silent — nobody notices it happening
  • Blocks other features or downstream systems

Where risk candidates come from

When a feature description gives you nothing to grip, walk the nine quality characteristics of ISO/IEC 25010 and ask “how could this feature fail here?”ISO/IEC 25010 product quality model, as published at iso25000.com. Sub-characteristics exist under each; the top level is enough as a prompt list.

The nine axes

  • Functional suitability — does it do the right thing, completely and correctly?
  • Performance efficiency — time, resources, capacity
  • Compatibility — co-existence, interoperability
  • Interaction capability — learnability, operability, error protection, inclusivity
  • Reliability — availability, fault tolerance, recoverability
  • Security — confidentiality, integrity, authenticity, resistance
  • Maintainability — modularity, analysability, testability
  • Flexibility — adaptability, scalability, installability
  • Safety — hazard warning, fail-safe behaviour

Fast prompts

  • What happens on the second attempt?
  • What if two people do this at once?
  • What if the network drops mid-way?
  • What does the user see when it fails?
  • Who can reach this without permission?
  • What happens at the boundary — zero, one, maximum, over?
  • What does the next system downstream receive?
  • Which failure would nobody notice for a month?

Product risk or project risk?

Product risk — you test it Project risk — you escalate it
Threatens the quality of what ships Threatens the ability to ship at all
Missing or wrong function, bad performance, security hole, unusable interface, data corruption Organisational, people, technical and supplier issues — hitting schedule, budget or scope
Becomes test cases Becomes a conversation with whoever owns the plan

The defence template

I’d test first. Impact is because , and likelihood is because .

Under pushback, hold one dial constant and argue the other — “same impact, but higher likelihood, because…” That is a claim someone can check, which is what makes it judgement rather than taste.

Traps

Don’t

  • Collapse both dials into “it’s critical”
  • Confuse a dramatic symptom with high impact
  • Rank by what is easy to find
  • Rank by what is easy to test
  • Rate impact without asking whose value
  • Treat the score as an answer rather than a starting position

Do

  • Name the stakeholder before calling something high impact
  • Look things up — analytics, defect history, logs
  • Say when a score is a guess, and what would sharpen it
  • Use test cost only as a tiebreaker between equal risks
  • Re-run the triage when the build or the deadline changes
  • Record residual risk you knowingly accepted
