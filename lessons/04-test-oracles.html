Lesson 4 · Foundations

Knowing what “correct” means

Test oracles: how to write a defensible expected result when the requirement goes quiet.

Plan: QA-002 · Test oracles and expected results · week 1 · must know · coverage map

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take one familiar user flow and write ten observable expected results. Each one will name the evidence behind it, expose any assumption you made, and become a question when two sources disagree. That is an interview-ready judgement, not a guess dressed up as a test case.

1. The requirement stops; testing does not

In lesson 3, an incomplete requirement was something you could catch in a review. Now imagine it reached testing unanswered. You sign in with the right password and land on the news feed. Is that correct? The story says only, “Registered users can sign in.”

An expected result is the observable outcome you have reason to anticipate after a particular action and starting condition. The phrase “have reason” carries the whole lesson. ISTQB describes test execution as comparing actual results with expected results, but its Foundation syllabus does not explain where those expectations come from. ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §1.4.1, p.19. The syllabus presupposes the expected result; QA-002 needs a source dedicated to finding one.

That reason comes from a test oracle: a principle or mechanism you use to recognise a possible problem. Michael Bolton’s important warning is that an oracle is fallible and context-dependent. It points to a problem; it does not prove one. Two sensible people can use different evidence and reach different expectations.Michael Bolton, Oracles, p.1. Barr et al. §2.2, Definition 2.4, p.509 gives the formal automation view: an oracle accepts or rejects an observed sequence as allowable behaviour.

The test oracle problem is the difficulty of distinguishing the desired behaviour from behaviour that may be incorrect. A precise formal specification can make that easy. Most teams have prose, tickets, help pages, conversations and an existing product instead. Barr and colleagues note that natural-language documentation is commonly partial and ambiguous — useful evidence, but not a complete judge. Barr, Harman, McMinn, Shahbaz & Yoo, The Oracle Problem in Software Testing: A Survey, §1, p.507 and §5.5, p.518.

Two source voices — keep them separate

ISTQB’s workflow: compare the actual result with a stated expected result. Bolton’s practitioner view: you often cannot know one definite answer in advance; an oracle may become visible while you test or even afterwards. Pre-write expectations when the evidence supports them. When it does not, write the uncertainty rather than manufacturing certainty.

2. Nine places to look for evidence

“The requirements” are only one oracle. Bolton’s nine guidewords widen the search. You do not need to memorise the acronym. You need to ask, what specific thing makes me expect this?

Evidence family Bolton’s guideword What you compare against
The product History An earlier version or previously observed behaviour
Product Another part of the same product
Familiarity A pattern you recognise from familiar failures
People and intent Claims A story, help page, contract, conversation or promise
Users’ expectations What a particular kind of user needs or reasonably anticipates
Purpose The job the feature exists to help someone complete
Image The reputation or identity the organisation wants to project
The outside world Comparable products Another product, service or non-software process worth comparing
Standards and statutes A relevant standard, rule, regulation or law

These categories overlap, and Bolton says that is fine. The category is a search prompt, not proof. “Claims” is weak on its own; “acceptance criterion AC-2 says the Account page opens” is evidence another person can inspect. Name the specific source. Bolton, Oracles, pp.1–2. His mnemonic is HICCUPPS(F): History, Image, Comparable Products, Claims, Users’ Expectations, Product, Purpose, Standards and Statutes, plus Familiarity.

3. Turn evidence into an expected result

Use five columns. The first two make the result executable; the next three make your judgement reviewable.

A thin fictional sign-in brief

C1: A registered user who enters the correct email and password reaches the Account page. C2: Wrong credentials show “Email or password not recognised.” C3: The form offers “Remember me.” Nothing else is specified.

Starting condition + action Expected result Specific oracle Assumption Question raised
Registered user submits correct credentials The Account page opens Claim C1 “Account page” is the default destination Should a deep link return the user to its original page?
User submits a wrong password “Email or password not recognised” appears Claim C2 The wording is identical for every invalid credential Is there a retry limit?
User submits with email empty The form stays open and identifies the missing email Required-field pattern on the registration form The sign-in form follows the same product pattern Should validation happen before or after submit?
User signs in with “Remember me” selected The user remains signed in after closing and reopening the browser The control label and its apparent purpose “Remember” survives a browser restart For how long, and on which devices?

Notice the first row is not just “sign-in works.” It names the condition, action and observable destination. It also refuses to hide the deep-link assumption. The fourth row is weaker: a label and a plausible purpose suggest the result, but they do not define duration. That row is useful precisely because it creates a decision.

Given , when , I expect because . I assumed ; confirm with .

4. Conflicting oracles are a result

Suppose the help page says “Remember me keeps you signed in for 30 days,” while the security lead says every session closes overnight. There is no honest single expected result yet. Bolton’s instruction is not to decide which important person wins; it is to make the disagreement visible. Your row should show both sources and ask for a decision.

Expectation: unresolved — 30 days according to Help article H-14; overnight according to the security lead.

Assumption: neither source automatically outranks the other.

Question: which session rule will the product promise, and which source must be updated?

Even apparently obvious oracles need context. Barr et al. §6 calls a crash a typical implicit sign of failure, then immediately notes that a crash can be desired in a system built to find crashes. The habit is not “doubt everything forever.” It is “state why this observation matters here.”

Choose the evidence, not the comfortable answer

Oracle 1A requirement says that after five failed sign-in attempts, access is blocked for fifteen minutes. The new build allows a sixth attempt. What is the strongest oracle?

Oracle 2 · deliberate trapThe cart total becomes negative after removing an item. The specification says nothing about it. What is the best first response?

Oracle 3The help page promises a 30-day remembered session. The security lead says sessions close overnight. What should the tester write?

Oracle 4Which line is an expected result you could defend to another person?

Self-review before you call it done
  • Every result is visible or otherwise observable — never “works.”
  • Every oracle names a specific artifact, person, behaviour or rule.
  • Every unsupported choice appears as an assumption or a question.
  • Conflicting evidence is visible; you did not silently choose a winner.
  • The ten rows advance through one flow instead of repeating one result.

Ten is the plan’s practice target, not proof of completeness. Oracles are heuristics; they do not tell you when you have found every relevant expectation.

Say it in an interview

If an interviewer asks, “How do you test when requirements are incomplete?”, avoid both extremes: do not say you stop, and do not say you invent the answer.

I expected because . I assumed ; I would confirm it with .

Then add: “If two credible oracles disagree, that disagreement is a test result. I record both and get the product decision made.”

Read this next

Primary source — Michael Bolton, Oracles, pp.1–3. It is the best short practical source for this lesson: three dense pages, about ten minutes. It supplies the guidewords and the warning that oracles are fallible; it does not supply a written expected-result template, which is why this lesson adds one.

Deeper source — Barr et al., The Oracle Problem in Software Testing: A Survey, especially §1, §2.2 Definition 2.4, §5.5, §6 and §7. Allow about 25 minutes for those sections. It is an automation survey, so use it to understand the problem, not as a method for writing your ten rows.
