Lesson 8 · Test management

The one-page test plan

What belongs in a test plan and — the harder half — what you can leave out without losing the argument.

Plan: QA-006 Test planning · week 3 · deliverable: a one-page test plan for an account permissions feature · coverage map

By the end of this lesson you will have written a one-page test plan for a real feature, and be able to say of every line why it earned its place — and of everything you cut, why the plan is still complete without it. Cutting is the part that gets judged.

The feature you are planning for

Teamspace, a B2B workspace app, is adding role-based permissions. Four roles: Owner (transfer ownership, delete workspace), Admin (invite people, change roles below Admin), Member (create and edit content), Viewer (read only). The same rules apply in the web UI and in a REST API — the server interface other software calls. The UI hides controls a user cannot use. Role changes are meant to take effect immediately, including for people already signed in.

You are the only tester. You have four days. Everything below plans this.

1. What a test plan is actually for

Not compliance. The syllabus is unusually direct about the real payoff: “Test planning guides the testers’ thinking and forces the testers to confront the future challenges related to risks, schedules, people, tools, costs, effort, etc. The process of preparing a test plan is a useful way to think through the efforts needed to achieve the test objectives.”ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §5.1.1. Learning objective FL-5.1.1 is K2 — you are expected to explain the purpose and content of a test plan, so this is a talking objective as much as a writing one.

The document is the residue of that thinking. §5.1.1 lists four jobs it does once written: it documents the means and schedule for achieving test objectives; helps ensure test activities will meet the established criteria; serves as a means of communication with team members and other stakeholders; and demonstrates that testing will adhere to the existing test policy and test strategy, or explains why it will deviate.

That last clause is worth pocketing. A plan that departs from house practice is not a broken plan — it is a plan that owes one sentence of explanation.

2. The seven things a test plan can contain

§5.1.1 gives a list. Read it as the set of questions a plan must have an answer to, not as a table of contents:

Syllabus heading The question it answers On a one-pager
Context of testing
scope, objectives, test basis		 What am I testing, against what, and what is out? Usually state explicitly; compress when already shared.
Assumptions and constraints What must be true for this plan to hold? Usually state explicitly; compress when already shared.
Stakeholders
roles, responsibilities, training		 Who decides, who signs off, who do I ask? One line. Names, not an org chart.
Communication
forms, frequency, templates		 Where do results and defects go? One line.
Risk register
product risks, project risks		 What could go wrong, ranked? Top 5. This is the spine.
Test approach
levels, types, techniques, deliverables, entry and exit criteria, independence, metrics, test data, environments, deviations 		How will I test, and when am I done? Summarise here; link supporting details.
Budget and schedule How much time, by when? One line.

The syllabus adds: “More details about the test plan and its content can be found in the ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-3 standard.”ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §5.1.1, final line. Worth knowing that this pointer is contested: parts 1–3 of ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119 were the subject of a public petition from the context-driven testing community asking for their withdrawal. I could not retrieve the petition text directly to quote it, so treat the detail as background, not as something to assert in an interview. Note what that sentence concedes: §5.1.1 is a summary of a much heavier standard. Nothing says you must ship the heavy version.

3. Why one page is not a lesser plan

Here the two source traditions in this course pull apart, and the disagreement is the useful part.

ISTQB treats the test plan as a document with a content list, and defers to a standard for more. Rapid Software Testing denies the premise. Michael Bolton: “a plan is the sum or intersection of strategy and logistics… The most important thing to note about this is that a plan is not a physical thing; it’s a set of ideas. Thus, it’s important to keep clear the difference between the plan and the planning documents.”Michael Bolton, What Should A Test Plan Contain?, DevelopSense, December 2008. Written as a reply to a reader’s question, which is why it is unusually concrete.

Asked how long the document should be, he gives a rule you can actually apply: “The final answer is ‘the very least expensive representation of the idea that can sufficiently store or communicate the idea.’” And the reason to care — “the more time we spend planning, the less time we spend interacting with the product.”

How to hold both at once

The seven areas are a checklist for your thinking. They are not a table of contents for your document. Cut the document; never cut the thinking. A one-pager where budget collapsed to “me, four days” has not skipped budget — it has answered it in four words, which is the least expensive representation that still communicates the idea.

Bolton offers one more test, which he credits to Cem Kaner, and it is the fastest way to decide how much to write: is this document a product — “generally designed to supply someone else with something that they want” — or a tool, “generally designed to supply ourselves with something that helps us to accomplish our own mission”? A plan for you and two developers is a tool. A plan going to an auditor is a product. Same feature, very different page count, and you should be able to say which one you were writing.

4. Exercise: what earns a place on the page?

You are drafting the Teamspace permissions plan. Audience: the two developers who built the feature, and the PM who signs off. Nobody external. Pick the items that belong on the one page — and leave out the ones that cost more than they return.

5. Entry and exit criteria: the part everyone fakes

Entry criteria define the preconditions for undertaking a given activity. The syllabus explains why they are worth two lines: “If entry criteria are not met, it is likely that the activity will prove to be more difficult, time-consuming, costly, and riskier.” Typical ones are available resources, available testing materials, and a stable enough feature to test — for example, quick checks of its main paths have passed.ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §5.1.3. In Agile teams these are usually called the Definition of Ready; exit criteria are the Definition of Done. Same idea, and the syllabus says so explicitly — use whichever word the interviewer used.

Exit criteria define what must be achieved to declare an activity completed. §5.1.3 gives two flavours: measures of thoroughness — achieved level of coverage, number of unresolved defects, defect density, number of failed checks — and binary yes/no criteria, such as planned tests have been executed, or all defects found are reported.

Then it says something most people find surprising, and it is the honest part: “Running out of time or budget can also be viewed as valid exit criteria. Even without other exit criteria being satisfied, it can be acceptable to end testing under such circumstances, if the stakeholders have reviewed and accepted the risk to go live without further testing.” The condition is the whole point. Stopping because time ran out is legitimate; stopping quietly because time ran out is not.

7. The deliverable, worked

Here is the plan those decisions produce. Every one of §5.1.1’s seven areas is answered; three of them collapsed to a single line.

Test plan — Teamspace account permissions · v1 · 11 Aug 2026

Scope. Role enforcement for Owner, Admin, Member and Viewer across the web UI and the REST API: invite, role change, ownership transfer, revoke. Out: SSO provisioning (unchanged this release), billing permissions (separate feature), load behaviour.

Assumptions and constraints. Seeded accounts for all four roles exist in staging by 11 Aug. One tester, four days. Staging shares auth config with production, so ownership-transfer tests run only inside seeded workspaces.

Risks, ranked. (1) API does not enforce what the UI hides — a Viewer calls the endpoint directly and succeeds. Impact high, likelihood medium. (2) A demoted Admin keeps privileges until their session expires. Impact high, likelihood medium. (3) An Admin can invite someone at a role above their own. Impact medium, likelihood medium. (4) The last Owner can leave, orphaning the workspace. Impact high, likelihood low. (5) Denial responses reveal whether a resource exists — 403 for an existing forbidden resource but 404 for a missing one. Impact low, likelihood medium.

Approach. Risk order. Risks 1–2 at API level first, because that is where the fastest signal is; UI after. A grid across every role and action for systematic coverage. Equivalence partitioning and boundary values on the invite email and role fields. One 60-minute focused session on ownership transfer, guided by a short charter, with notes attached. No automation this cycle; risk 1 becomes an automated API check next sprint.

Entry. Four seeded role accounts available; acceptance criteria reviewed; feature deployed to staging with quick checks of its main paths passing.

Exit. All planned tests executed; no unresolved defect permits an unauthorised role action; risks 1–3 each covered by at least one executed test; anything unfinished listed with its residual risk and accepted in writing by the PM.

Schedule and people. Test 11–14 Aug; sign-off Dana (PM); defects in Linear, daily summary in #teamspace-qa.

The ranking follows Lesson 1’s 1–3 matrix: the scores are 6, 6, 4, 3 and 2. Risks 1 and 2 tie; risk 1 goes first because a direct API check gives fast signal across all four roles. Risk 1 falls under the top category in the current OWASP list — A01:2025, Broken Access Control. OWASP Top 10:2025, category A01. The list was revised in 2025; broken access control remains at number one. Naming the category in a risk line is a cheap way to show a reviewer the risk is not your personal hunch. A hidden button is not a permission check.

Risk 5 is not saying that 403 is always wrong. HTTP explicitly allows a server to return 404 when it wants to hide that a forbidden resource exists. The leak happens when an unauthorised caller can distinguish an existing ID from a missing one by comparing the responses. RFC 9110 §15.5.4: an origin server that wishes to hide a forbidden resource may respond with 404 instead.

Now write yours. Take a feature you know — or ask me for a fresh one — and write the seven blocks above on one page. Paste it back and I will do two passes: which of the seven you answered without noticing, and which line a developer would still have to ask you about. The second pass is the one that improves the page.

8. Retrieval

Recall 1Your permissions plan is for you and the two developers on your team — nobody outside. Kaner’s tool-or-product question decides what next?

Recall 2The PM asks when testing will be done. Which answer is the strongest exit criterion?

Recall 3Your draft has run to three pages and must come down to one. Which cut costs you least?

The interview move

“Show me a test plan you wrote” is really asking whether you can defend your omissions. Lead with the audience, then the compression:

This plan was a for , so I kept it to one page. I worked through all seven content areas; and collapsed to a line each because . The section I would defend hardest is , because .

Saying “I thought through all seven and compressed three” is a different claim from “I wrote a short plan.” The first is judgement; the second is a page count.
