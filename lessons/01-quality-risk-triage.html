What to test first, when you can’t test everything

The QA mindset, and the two dials that turn a hunch about quality into a ranking you can defend out loud.

Plan: covers QA-001 QA mindset and quality risk and QA-003 Test prioritization · weeks 1–2 · coverage map

By the end of this lesson you will be able to take a feature, list what could go wrong, score each item on likelihood and impact separately, and state the evidence behind each score. That is the raw material of every “how would you test this?” interview answer.

1. The question testing actually answers

Most people outside the discipline think a tester’s job is to answer does it work? That question has no useful answer, because “work” is not a property of software. James Bach and Michael Bolton put the real job like this:

Testing’s purpose is to discover the status of the product and any threats to its value, so that our clients can make informed decisions about it. Rapid Software Testing — developsense.com

Two words there do the work. Threats — you are hunting for what could go wrong, not confirming what goes right. Value — and value is never abstract. The same methodology defines quality as value to some person who mattersJerry Weinberg’s original phrase was “value to some person”. Bach added who matters, which is the part that makes it usable: it forces you to name a stakeholder before you can call anything a bug.. So a bug is not a deviation from a document. A bug is something that threatens value for a person whose opinion counts.

The mindset shift

Stop asking does this work? Start asking how could this fail, who would be hurt, and how badly? Everything else in this lesson is machinery for answering that efficiently.

2. You are always sampling — the only choice is how well

The ISTQB syllabus’ second testing principle is blunt: exhaustive testing is impossible. Testing everything is infeasible except in trivial cases, so the syllabus points instead at “test techniques, test case prioritization, and risk-based testing.”ISTQB CTFL v4.0, §1.3, principle 2. Note this is not a claim about budgets. An infinite budget still would not let you test every input, every sequence, on every device.

This matters more than it sounds. It means every test effort that has ever existed was a sample of the possible tests. You are not choosing between complete testing and partial testing — you are choosing between a thoughtful sample and an accidental one. “I ran out of time” is not a defence; it is the normal condition of the job.

The good news is in principle 4: defects cluster together. A small number of components usually contain most of the defects. Faults are not spread evenly, so a smart sample genuinely does beat a random one. Prioritisation is not a compromise — it is the technique.

3. Risk is two dials, not one

Here is the definition the industry hires against. ISTQB defines a risk as “a potential event, hazard, threat, or situation whose occurrence causes an adverse effect”, characterised by two factors:

Factor The question it answers Evidence you can cite
Likelihood How probable is this failure? Code churn, new vs. mature component, past defect history, complexity, unfamiliar tech, environment differences
Impact How much harm if it happens? Users affected, money lost, data lost, legal or safety exposure, reputational damage, whether a workaround exists
Risk level How urgently should this be treated? The combination of the two above

Selecting, prioritising and managing test activity on this basis has a name you should use in interviews: risk-based testing.ISTQB CTFL v4.0, §5.2.1 and the glossary definition of risk-based testing: “the test approach, in which test activities are selected, prioritized, and managed based on risk analysis and risk control.”

The one habit that separates candidates

Score the two dials separately, and out loud. Weak answers collapse into a single feeling: “that’s critical, I’d test it first.” Strong answers split it: “impact is high because a locked-out user cannot self-recover; likelihood is high because email deliverability behaves differently in production than in staging.” Same conclusion, but now it is arguable — and being arguable is what makes it judgement rather than a guess.

A trap worth naming now: impact is not severity of the symptom. A crash on a page nobody uses is a dramatic symptom with low impact. A silently wrong total on an invoice is an undramatic symptom with very high impact. Judge harm to the person who matters, not how loud the failure is.

4. Product risk vs. project risk

ISTQB splits risk in two, and interviewers use the split to check whether you know where your job ends.

Product risk Project risk
Threatens The quality of the thing you ship The team’s ability to ship it
Examples Missing function, wrong calculation, poor performance, security hole, unusable interface Organisational, people, technical and supplier issues — affecting schedule, budget or scope
Your response Test it — this is what shapes your test plan Escalate it — you raise it, someone else owns it

Both are real and both belong in a risk register. But only product risk tells you what to test. Mixing them up in an interview reads as not knowing what testing is for.

Triage: two days before release

A small e-commerce team ships “Reset your password” on Friday. It is Wednesday. You are the only tester, and you will not get through everything.

Score each risk on both dials. Watch the matrix fill in as you go, then check your triage. There is no single right answer — being one step away from the reference is marked defensible. What is being trained here is the habit of moving two dials independently.

Ties are informative. If two risks land on the same score, the ranking question becomes: which is cheaper to test, and which would be harder to detect in production if you skipped it?
