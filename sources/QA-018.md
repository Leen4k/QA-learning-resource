## a. Does the named source cover this?

No. It teaches nothing about this topic.

`roadmap.sh/qa` is a node graph. I fetched it: navigation links, GitHub-star counts, social
buttons, and the tagline "Steps to follow in order to become a modern QA Engineer in 2026."
No article text. The per-node blurbs are the only prose. I pulled the node's source file from
the roadmap's content repo (`compatibility@RD0vRC-OL2ReYLT1leJMs.md`): **two sentences and
one Guru99 link**, quoted in full below. That is the entire treatment of this task's first
half. For the responsive half there is no matching node — the nearest file,
`responsive-vs-adaptive@6cGPxfJ9ipij40hnmckxV.md`, is a *design-approach* distinction, not a
testing one. Nothing in the roadmap mentions breakpoints, viewports, or screen sizes. Against
the DELIVERABLE it gives a learner zero: no matrix columns, no selection rule for which
browsers earn a row, no stopping rule, no worked example, and nothing on what to check once a
responsive page is open.

**Also worth flagging:** ISTQB does not teach this either. I read CTFL v4.0 §2.2.2 directly
(ASTQB-hosted official PDF, p.28). "Compatibility" and "Portability" appear exactly once each
— as bullets in a seven-item list of ISO/IEC 25010 quality characteristics. "Browser,"
"device," and "responsive" do not appear in §2.2 at all. Foundation gives you the *vocabulary*
for this task and none of the *method*.

## b. Best primary source

**MDN's Testing module** — free, maintained by Mozilla, and the only free authoritative
document I found that answers the deliverable's actual question ("which browsers go in the
matrix, and why those?") rather than just defining the term. Two pages do the work.
**`Testing/Testing_strategies`** is the matrix-building page: the selection principle
("important" = "commonly used among the target audience"), the A/B/C support classification
with a stated testing action per grade, two named data sources for populating it (analytics,
Statcounter), and the real-device-vs-emulator tradeoff. **`Core/CSS_layout/Responsive_Design`**
is the "test one responsive page" page: breakpoints, mobile-first, and the viewport meta tag —
including *why* omitting it breaks your media queries, a ready-made first exercise.

**Stability caveat — read before building a lesson on it.** The module carries a note: "This
module was originally dedicated in its entirety to cross-browser testing, but we are in the
process of refocusing it to cover testing in general." The old
`Learn/Tools_and_testing/Cross_browser_testing/` URLs are already redirects. Archive anything
the lesson depends on.

## c. What it leaves out

- **No matrix artifact.** MDN gives the A/B/C *principle* but never shows a filled-in grid —
  no columns, no rows, no example. The learner must invent the format, which is exactly the
  deliverable. The lesson has to supply the template.
- **No sizing or stopping rule.** Nothing says whether "small" is 4 rows or 40, or what
  coverage you concede when you stop.
- **No responsive test checklist.** MDN says what responsive design *is* and how to emulate
  screen widths; it never lists what to look at once you're there. Orientation, text zoom / OS
  font scaling, `prefers-reduced-motion`, `prefers-color-scheme` and safe-area insets are all
  absent as testable axes.
- **Nothing on defect reporting.** No guidance on which environment fields (device, OS version,
  browser version, viewport width, DPR) a cross-platform bug report needs to be reproducible.
- **The A/B/C scheme is undated.** It descends from Yahoo's Graded Browser Support, written
  for a pre-evergreen web. MDN neither says so nor asks whether "B-grade: older/less capable
  browsers" still describes anything in 2026.
- **No bridge to QA vocabulary.** MDN never uses "compatibility testing" or "portability," so
  a learner won't connect this to the ISO/IEC 25010 names their org uses. CTFL §2.2.2 supplies
  those two words and nothing more.
- **No exercises.** I checked the module index: no Assessment or Active Learning pages exist.

## d. Credible disagreement

**1. Michael Bolton, *Pairwise Testing* (v1.5, Nov 2007) — against the artifact itself.**
A browser × device matrix is a combinatorial coverage model, and Bolton's postscript is a
direct warning about what such grids are *for*: a project manager reacted to his pairwise
table by saying it would "impress the auditors." He quotes Cem Kaner's objection that people
"go through the motions of entering the variables' values into the input fields but they
don't then continue with the program to see how it uses those values." Applied here: a tester
can tick all twelve cells of a browser matrix having learned nothing, because the matrix says
*where* to look and never *what for*.

**2. James Bach's Heuristic Test Strategy Model (v6.0, 2024-02-08) — a different structure.**
HTSM has no "browser matrix." It handles this territory as *risk areas to think about*:
Product Elements → **Platform** (External Hardware, External Software, Embedded Components,
Product Footprint) and Quality Criteria → **Compatibility** (Application, Operating System,
Hardware, Backward). Note what a browser matrix structurally cannot hold but HTSM does:
Operations → **Environment**, "the physical environment in which the product operates,
including such elements as noise, light, and distractions." A contrast ratio that fails in
sunlight passes every cell of every grid.

**3. Brad Frost, "Support vs. Optimization" (2011-12-20) — against the binary framing.**
Support and optimization are different commitments; you can "support lesser platforms while
still optimizing for the best of the best." Don't overstate this into a clash with MDN — it is
broadly *compatible* with the C-grade. It is a corrective to how teams read a matrix in
practice: as a list of browsers that count and an implied list that doesn't.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

- **Delete the viewport meta tag.** A mechanical, always-reproducible defect that MDN explains:
  without `<meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width,initial-scale=1">` a mobile
  browser reports ~980px, so a 480px breakpoint never fires and the user gets a zoomed-out
  desktop layout. Best possible first exercise — remove it, observe, restore.
- **The `100vh` trap.** MDN's CSS length docs record that `vh` "is equivalent to `lvh`," the
  *large* viewport — sized as though browser UI were retracted, so a `height: 100vh` element
  has its bottom cut off whenever the address bar shows. CSS added `svh`/`lvh`/`dvh` because
  of this: a standards-level fix to a real defect class, reproducible on one page.
- **Grade a real analytics table.** MDN gives the A/B/C definitions and names the data sources
  (Google Analytics, Statcounter) but joins them to nothing. Hand the learner a browser-share
  table; make them assign grades and defend the cut line — that *is* the matrix exercise.
- **Bolton's "impress the auditors"** (§d) reframes the whole deliverable in one sentence: who
  is this grid for, and what happens if every cell comes back green?
- **Gap:** no citable public post-mortem of a compatibility defect reaching production. A war
  story must come from the learner's workplace or be an explicit hypothetical.

## Verbatim quotes

**roadmap.sh QA, `compatibility` node — the complete content:**
- "Compatibility is nothing but the capability of existing or living together. Compatibility Testing is a type of Software testing to check whether your software is capable of running on different hardware, operating systems, applications, network environments or Mobile devices."
- Plus one link: `[@article@What is Compatibility Testing? Forward & Backward Example](https://www.guru99.com/compatibility-testing.html)`

**MDN, `Testing/Testing_strategies`:**
- §"Choosing which browsers and devices to test": "Since you can't test every combination of browser and device, it's enough that you ensure your site works on the most important ones. In practical applications, 'important' often means 'commonly used among the target audience.'"
- Same §: "1. A-grade: Common/modern browsers — Known to be capable. Test thoroughly and provide full support. 2. B-grade: Older/less capable browsers — known not to be capable. Test, and provide a more basic experience that gives full access to core information and services. 3. C-grade: Rare/unknown browsers — don't test, but assume they are capable. Serve the full site, which should work, at least with the fallbacks provided by our defensive coding."
- §"Physical devices": "It is generally better to have a real device running the browser you want to test — this provides the greatest accuracy in terms of behavior and overall user experience."
- §"Using analytics" names Google Analytics; §"Browser statistics" names Statcounter.

**MDN, `Testing/Introduction`:**
- §"What is cross-browser testing?": "Cross-browser testing is the practice of ensuring that a website works across various browsers and devices."
- "Remember that you are not your users — just because your site works on your MacBook Pro or high-end Galaxy Nexus, doesn't mean it will work for all your users!"
- §"Testing/discovery": "1. Test it in a couple of stable browsers on your system, like Firefox, Safari, Chrome, or Edge. 2. Do some lo-fi accessibility testing, such as trying to use your site with only the keyboard, or using your site via a screen reader to see if it is navigable. 3. Test on a mobile platform, such as Android or iOS."

**MDN, `Core/CSS_layout/Responsive_Design` and `Web/CSS/length`:**
- §"The viewport meta tag": "By setting `width=device-width` you are overriding a mobile device's default, like the iPhone's default `width=980px`, with the actual width of the device. Without it, your responsive design with breakpoints and media queries may not work as intended on mobile browsers. If you've got a narrow screen layout that kicks in at 480px viewport width or less, but the device is saying it is 980px wide, that user will not see your narrow screen layout."
- §"Media queries": "If using breakpoints, best practices encourage defining media query breakpoints with relative units rather than absolute sizes of an individual device."
- `Web/CSS/length`: "`vh` is equivalent to `lvh`, representing the viewport-percentage length unit based on the large viewport size." / "The allowance for the different viewport sizes is in response to browser interfaces expanding and retracting dynamically and hiding and showing the content underneath."
