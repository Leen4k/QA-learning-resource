## a. Does the named source cover this?

No. Same URL, same verdict as QA-006 and QA-007. Fetched this session it returns
**HTTP 403 Forbidden** to automated clients; a human browser gets a certification
storefront — no template, no field list, no steps-to-reproduce guidance, no

the five reports. Its only value is the "Download Materials" links.

The document behind those links does cover this task, exactly. The chapter 5
learning-objective list on p.46 ends with **"FL-5.5.1 (K3) Prepare a defect
report."** K3 = apply, not recall — this task's deliverable stated as an exam
objective. The plan named the right organisation and the wrong document.

## b. Best primary source

Two documents; neither is sufficient alone. Split them along the two halves of the
deliverable.

1. **ISTQB CTFL v4.0 §5.5 Defect Management (pp.55–56)** — the authoritative field
   list. About 1.5 pages: why a defect management process exists, three objectives
   of a defect report, and an eleven-item content list for "a defect report logged
   during dynamic testing" — unique identifier; title; date + issuing organization
   + author and role; test object and test environment; context; description of the
   failure enabling reproduction; expected and actual results; **severity**;
   **priority to fix**; status; references. Read **§1.2.3 (p.17)** first for error →
   defect → failure, or the learner will use "bug" for all three in one report.
2. **Mozilla Bug Writing Guidelines — https://bugzilla.mozilla.org/page.cgi?id=bug-writing.html**
   — the reproducibility half. Free, live, maintained by a project that receives
   thousands of reports from strangers. Headings: *How to report a bug / Writing a
   clear summary / Writing precise steps to reproduce / Providing additional
   information / Advanced / Finding the correct product and component / General
   Outline of a bug Report*. It has the two things §5.5 lacks: good-vs-bad example
   titles, and the rule to separate observation from speculation.

For the severity-vs-priority half, §5.5 gives you two adjacent bullets and no
definitions. Use Mozilla's live field rubric (§e item 4) to make it concrete.

## c. What it leaves out

1. **No template and no example.** §5.5 ends by punting: templates and example
   defect reports "can be found in the ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-3 standard" — paywalled.
   The one official worked example is in the sample exam, which the syllabus never
   cross-references (§e).
2. **Severity and priority are never defined or contrasted.** Two bullets, one
   parenthetical. Nothing on who assigns which, on what scale, or on the case this
   deliverable exists to teach — low severity, top priority (a typo on the checkout
   button) and its inverse.
3. **Nothing on how to make a report reproducible.** "Description of the failure to
   enable reproduction" is the entire instruction. No word on step granularity,
   preconditions, trimming irrelevant steps, or which environment details matter —
   precisely the learner's stated want.
4. **Triage is thin.** A workflow (log → analyze and classify → decide a response →
   close) and a status list, but no triage meeting, no rules for who decides,
   nothing on duplicates or rejected reports.
5. **Non-reproducible defects.** §5.5 assumes reproduction succeeds.
6. **Nothing on tone**, or on the report as an act of persuasion.

## d. Credible disagreement

**RST vs ISTQB on what a bug *is*.** ISTQB §1.2.3 locates the defect in the
artifact: defects live "in documentation, such as a requirements specification or
a test script, in source code, or in a supporting artifact such as a build file."
Michael Bolton, *You've Got Issues* (2011-01-31, lightly edited 2025-07-14,
https://developsense.com/blog/2011/01/youve-got-issues) locates it in a relationship:
"a bug is anything about that product that threatens its value to people who
matter." Consequence: under ISTQB you fill a severity field; under RST the argument
for *why this matters and to whom* is the report itself and cannot be delegated to
a dropdown. Bolton also insists on a category chapter 5 has no slot for — the
**issue**, "anything that threatens the value of the project."

**Kaner: the report is advocacy, not a record.** Cem Kaner, *Bug Advocacy* (Quality
Week 2002, https://kaner.com/pdfs/BugAdvocacy.pdf — © 2002 Kaner, all rights
reserved; link, do not reproduce slides): *"Think of a bug report as a tool that you
use to sell the company on the idea of spending time and energy to fix a bug."*
ISTQB's three objectives are informational. Kaner's premise is that most bugs
customers complain about were found in testing and not fixed, so the report's job is
to win an argument. The deck also covers what §5.5 omits (slides *"Objection,
objection: Non-reproducible errors"*, *"Non-Reproducible Errors: Examples of
Conditions Often Missed"*) — titles from a text extraction, not yet checked against
the slide images.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

1. **ISTQB Sample Exam set A, Question 38 — the only official worked example of
   FL-5.5.1** (both PDFs read this session; ISTQB documents hosted by ASTQB, not an
   ASTQB summary page). Questions v1.0 p.20 of 30, https://astqb.org/assets/documents/ISTQB_CTFL_Sample-Exam-Questions_v4.0.pdf ;
   rationale in Answers v1.0 p.24 of 34, https://astqb.org/assets/documents/ISTQB_CTFL_Sample-Exam-Answers_v4.0.pdf .
   A complete narrative defect report about an application hanging on the input
   `Test input: $ä`, returned by developers as not reproducible; the question asks
   what critical information is missing. Answer **(c) Test environment and test
   item**. Best single exercise here: show the report text alone, ask what is
   missing, *then* reveal the four options. Note the report is weak in other ways
   the exam does not mark down (no numbered steps; priority asserted by reporter).
2. **RIMGEN / RIMGEA — https://bbst.courses/rimgen/** — six steps for turning an
   observation into a report: Replicate, Isolate ("identify the shortest number of
   critical steps necessary"), Maximize, Generalize, Externalize ("highlight who
   will be affected by the bug"), Neutral tone. Fills gaps (c)(3) and (c)(6). Page
   carries "© 2026 ALTOM CONSULTING"; BBST v3.0 lecture material is said to be
   CC-BY — verify before reproducing.
3. **Simon Tatham, *How to Report Bugs Effectively*** (© 1999, OpenContent Licence,
   https://www.chiark.greenend.org.uk/~sgtatham/bugs.html). Ten sections, each a
   named failure mode ("It doesn't work.", "Show me.", "Works for me. So what goes
   wrong?", "That's funny, it did it a moment ago."). Adapt the headings into an
   anti-pattern checklist for reviewing the learner's own five reports.
4. **Mozilla severity and priority as a live rubric** —
   https://wiki.mozilla.org/BMO/UserGuide/BugFields . Severity answers *how bad*
   (S1 catastrophic → S4 trivial); priority answers *when it gets fixed* (P1 current
   release cycle → P5 will not fix). Two questions, two owners, in a tracker the
   learner can go and read — the cleanest way to make the deliverable's second half
   concrete.
5. **A defect story from the syllabus: there isn't one.** §5.5 has no narrative at
   all. Q38's hang is the closest thing ISTQB publishes.

## Verbatim quotes

**§5.5, p.55** — "Since one of the major test objectives is to find defects, an established defect management process is essential." … "At a minimum, the defect management process includes a workflow for handling individual anomalies from their discovery to their closure and rules for their classification. The workflow typically comprises activities to log the reported anomalies, analyze and classify them, decide on a suitable response such as to fix or keep it as it is and finally to close the defect report."

**§5.5, p.55** — "Typical defect reports have the following objectives: Provide those responsible for handling and resolving reported defects with sufficient information to resolve the issue; Provide a means of tracking the quality of the work product; Provide ideas for improvement of the development and test process"

**§5.5, pp.55–56** — "A defect report logged during dynamic testing typically includes: Unique identifier / Title with a short summary of the anomaly being reported / Date when the anomaly was observed, issuing organization, and author, including their role / Identification of the test object and test environment / Context of the defect (e.g., test case being run, test activity being performed, SDLC phase, and other relevant information such as the test technique, checklist or test data being used) / Description of the failure to enable reproduction and resolution including the steps that detected the anomaly, and any relevant test logs, database dumps, screenshots, or recordings / Expected results and actual results / Severity of the defect (degree of impact) on the interests of stakeholders or requirements / Priority to fix / Status of the defect (e.g., open, deferred, duplicate, waiting to be fixed, awaiting confirmation testing, re-opened, closed, rejected) / References (e.g., to the test case)"

**§5.5, p.56** — "Document templates for a defect report and example defect reports can be found in the ISO/IEC/IEEE 29119-3 standard, which refers to defect reports as incident reports."

**§1.2.3, p.17** — "Human beings make errors (mistakes), which produce defects (faults, bugs), which in turn may result in failures." … "Some defects will always result in a failure if executed, while others will only result in a failure in specific circumstances, and some may never result in a failure."

**Chapter 5 LOs, p.46** — "5.5 Defect Management — FL-5.5.1 (K3) Prepare a defect report"

**Sample Exam A, Q38, Questions p.20** — "What critical information is MISSING from this test report that would have been useful for the developers? a) Expected result and actual result b) References and defect status c) Test environment and test item d) Priority and severity" — rationale, Answers p.24: "c) Is correct. We do not know in which test environment the anomaly was detected, and we also do not know which application (and its version) is affected"

**Mozilla Bug Writing Guidelines** — "A good summary should quickly and uniquely identify a bug report. It should explain the problem, not your suggested solution." (good example: "Cancelling a File Copy dialog crashes File Manager") … "Steps to reproduce are the most important part of any bug report. If a developer is able to reproduce the bug, the bug is very likely to be fixed." … "Clearly separate facts (observations) from speculations." … "Open a new bug report for each issue!"

**Mozilla BMO/UserGuide/BugFields** — severity "S1 (Catastrophic) Blocks development/testing, may impact more than 25% of users, causes data loss, likely dot release driver, and no workaround available" … "S4 (Small/Trivial) minor significance, cosmetic issues, low or no impact to users"; priority "P1 Fix in the current release cycle" … "P5 Will not fix, but will accept a patch".

**Tatham 1999** — "The aim of a bug report is to enable the programmer to see the program failing in front of them." … "Providing your own diagnosis might be helpful sometimes, but always state the symptoms. The diagnosis is an optional extra, and not an alternative."

**Bolton 2011/2025** — "a bug is anything about that product that threatens its value to people who matter"; "an issue is anything that threatens the value of the project, and in particular the test effort."

**Kaner 2002, slide 4** — "Think of a bug report as a tool that you use to sell the company on the idea of spending time and energy to fix a bug."
