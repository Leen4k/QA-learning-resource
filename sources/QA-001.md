> before it ships in a lesson.

- **Verdict:** WRONG-DOCUMENT

## a. Does the named source cover this?

No. roadmap.sh/qa is a node graph, not a document. Fetched this session, the
page body is navigation, share buttons, GitHub/Discord counters, a footer, and
one line of framing: *"Steps to follow in order to become a modern QA Engineer
in 2026."* There is no prose on mindset, risk, users, or evidence.

The per-node blurbs live in the content repo and are 2–4 sentences. The
relevant node, `learn-the-fundamentals`, is **two sentences in full**:

> "The fundamentals of QA cover the core concepts, terminology, and practices
> every software tester needs to know. This includes what quality assurance is,
> common testing techniques and approaches, test management tools, and how QA
> integrates into software development lifecycles."

That is a table of contents for a topic, not a treatment of it. It defines
nothing, argues nothing, and contains the word "risk" zero times. A learner
sent here cannot write either half of the deliverable. Its only real use is as
a **checklist of topic names** to reassure a beginner that the syllabus is
finite — keep it as an orientation link, not the teaching source.

## b. Best primary source

ISTQB CTFL v4.0. It is free, versioned, stable, and — unusually for this plan —
covers *both halves* of the deliverable in two separate chapters:

**"What QA protects" → Chapter 1 (180 min):**
- §1.1 What is Testing? + §1.1.1 Test Objectives (p.15) — the nine-bullet objectives list
- §1.2.1 Testing's Contributions to Success (p.16) — incl. the "indirect representation" of users
- §1.2.2 Testing and Quality Assurance (pp.16–17) — the QA/QC distinction
- §1.2.3 Errors, Defects, Failures, and Root Causes (p.17) — the error→defect→failure chain
- §1.3 Testing Principles (pp.17–18) — the seven principles; #1, #2 and #7 are the mindset
- §1.5.1–1.5.3 (pp.21–22) — skills, whole-team approach, independence

**"Five risks that matter" → §5.2 Risk Management (pp.50–52):**
- §5.2.1 Risk Definition and Risk Attributes (p.50) — likelihood × impact = risk level
- §5.2.2 Project Risks and Product Risks (p.51) — the taxonomy the learner should sort by
- §5.2.3 Product Risk Analysis (p.51) / §5.2.4 Product Risk Control (p.52)

⚠️ **Trap in the task wording.** The deliverable says "what QA protects," but
§1.2.2 states flatly that testing *is not* QA — testing is quality **control**.
Taken literally, the deliverable asks about the process-improvement function,
which is almost certainly not what the learner means. Either reword the
deliverable to "what testing protects" or teach the distinction explicitly and
make the note answer both. Do not let the learner write a note that the
syllabus's own chapter 1 would mark wrong.

## c. What it leaves out

1. **Anything web-specific.** §5.2.2 gives a generic product-risk list (missing
   functionality, incorrect calculations, runtime errors, poor architecture,
   inadequate response time, security vulnerabilities). Nothing about auth,
   sessions, browsers, latency, third-party JS, or data migration. The learner
   must supply the web domain themselves — see §e.
2. **How to *write* a risk.** §5.2.3 says to identify and assess risks but never
   shows the sentence form. A risk is not "security" — it's *"an authenticated
   user can read another user's records by changing an ID in the URL."* The
   syllabus never models that difference, and it is the whole skill.
3. **ISO 25010, which it leans on.** §5.2.2 defers the product-quality model to
   ISO 25010 — paywalled. The free HTSM list (§e) covers the same ground.
4. **Scoring.** §5.2.1 defines likelihood and impact but gives no scale, no
   matrix, no worked example. "Qualitative approach… using a risk matrix" is
   the entire treatment.
5. **Evidence.** The learner's stated want includes "evidence"; chapter 1 does
   not use the word. Closest is §1.1's verification/validation split and
   §1.4.4's traceability.

## d. Credible disagreement

**Rapid Software Testing (Bach & Bolton) vs ISTQB — they disagree about what
"quality" refers to, and it changes what the learner's note should say.**

ISTQB treats quality as conformance to defined product characteristics (ISO
25010) and QA as a *process* discipline: "QA is a process-oriented, preventive
approach… It works on the basis that if a good process is followed correctly,
then it will generate a good product" (§1.2.2). RST rejects the premise that
good process yields good product. Its stated definition is relational:
**"Quality is always value to some person who matters"** (developsense.com/rst-approach),
and testing's purpose is **"to discover the status of the product and any
threats to its value, so that our clients can make informed decisions about
it"** (satisfice.com/rapid-testing-methodology).

The sharpest line for this task is RST's premise 8: **"Testers accept
responsibility for the quality of their work, although they cannot control the
quality of the product."** That is a direct answer to "what does QA protect"
and it is *not* ISTQB's answer. RST also holds that "a test is an activity; it
is performance, not artifacts" (premise 4) — against a mindset lesson that
grades the learner on documents produced.

Don't blend them. Honest framing: ISTQB gives the learner a shared vocabulary
and a defensible risk taxonomy; RST tells them quality is not a property of the
product but a relationship, so "five risks that matter" is unanswerable until
you say *matter to whom*. The deliverable is stronger if each of the five risks
names a person who loses something.

Fair to ISTQB: §1.3 principle 7 (absence-of-defects fallacy) and principle 6
(testing is context dependent, citing Kaner) concede much of RST's ground.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

- **HTSM v6.0 "Quality Criteria Categories", Bach** — https://www.developsense.com/resource/htsm.pdf (read this session, p.5). Ten categories — Capability, Reliability, Usability, **Charisma**, Security, Scalability, Compatibility, Performance, Installability, Development — each with sub-bullets. Bach explicitly frames them as risk areas: *"You can also think of them as categories of product risk."* **This is the single best scaffold for the deliverable**: have the learner pick five categories and write one concrete web risk under each. Charisma and Development have no ISTQB equivalent and reliably produce the most interesting answers.
- **HTSM p.2, "Risk Testing" technique** — the four-question drill, verbatim: *"1. What kinds of problems could the product have? 2. Which kinds matter most? 3. How would you detect them if they were there? 4. Make a list of interesting problems and design tests specifically to reveal them."* Question 2 *is* the deliverable. Question 3 is the natural follow-on exercise.
- **OWASP Top 10:2025** — https://owasp.org/Top10/2025/ — a pre-built, authoritative risk register for a web product. Current order fetched this session: A01 Broken Access Control, A02 Security Misconfiguration, A03 Software Supply Chain Failures, A04 Cryptographic Failures, A05 Injection, A06 Insecure Design, A07 Authentication Failures, A08 Software or Data Integrity Failures, A09 Security Logging and Alerting Failures, A10 Mishandling of Exceptional Conditions. **Note for the whole repo: 2025 has superseded 2021 — `sources/QA-006.md` still cites A01:2021 and its CVE counts.** I did not verify 2025 incidence figures; the landing page carries none.
- **danluu/post-mortems** — https://github.com/danluu/post-mortems — a curated collection of public incident write-ups, ideal for making risk concrete. Strong candidates surfaced this session: GitLab 2017 (admin deleted the wrong directory, six hours of data lost), Amazon S3 2017 (typo removed more servers than intended), Facebook 2021 (backbone router config change → global outage). Each maps cleanly onto an HTSM category and shows a risk that a features-only test plan would never have caught. Re-read the individual post-mortems before quoting — I read the repo index, not the underlying reports.

## Verbatim quotes

**§1.1, p.15** — "Software that does not work correctly can lead to many problems, including loss of money, time or business reputation, and, in extreme cases, even injury or death. Software testing assesses software quality and helps reducing the risk of software failure in operation."

**§1.2.1, p.16** — "Testing provides users with indirect representation on the development project. Testers ensure that their understanding of users' needs are considered throughout the development lifecycle."

**§1.2.2, pp.16–17** — "While people often use the terms 'testing' and 'quality assurance' (QA) interchangeably, testing and QA are not the same. Testing is a form of quality control (QC)." … "QA is a process-oriented, preventive approach that focuses on the implementation and improvement of processes. It works on the basis that if a good process is followed correctly, then it will generate a good product. QA applies to both the development and testing processes, and is the responsibility of everyone on a project."

**§1.2.3, p.17** — "Human beings make errors (mistakes), which produce defects (faults, bugs), which in turn may result in failures. Humans make errors for various reasons, such as time pressure, complexity of work products, processes, infrastructure or interactions, or simply because they are tired or lack adequate training."

**§1.3, p.17** — "1. Testing shows the presence, not the absence of defects." … "2. Exhaustive testing is impossible. Testing everything is not feasible except in trivial cases."

**§1.3, p.18** — "7. Absence-of-defects fallacy. It is a fallacy (i.e., a misconception) to expect that software verification will ensure the success of a system. Thoroughly testing all the specified requirements and fixing all the defects found could still produce a system that does not fulfill the users' needs and expectations."

**§1.5.1, p.21** — "Testers are often the bearers of bad news. It is a common human trait to blame the bearer of bad news. This makes communication skills crucial for testers."

**§5.2.1, p.50** — "Risk is a potential event, hazard, threat, or situation whose occurrence causes an adverse effect. A risk can be characterized by two factors: • Risk likelihood – the probability of the risk occurrence (greater than zero and less than one) • Risk impact (harm) – the consequences of this occurrence"

**§5.2.2, p.51** — "Product risks are related to the product quality characteristics… Examples of product risks include: missing or wrong functionality, incorrect calculations, runtime errors, poor architecture, inefficient algorithms, inadequate response time, poor user experience, security vulnerabilities. Product risks, when they occur, may result in various negative consequences, including: • User dissatisfaction • Loss of revenue, trust, reputation • Damage to third parties • High maintenance costs, overload of the helpdesk • Criminal penalties"

**§5.2.3, p.51** — "the goal of product risk analysis is to provide an awareness of product risk in order to focus the testing effort in a way that minimizes the residual level of product risk."

**HTSM v6.0, p.5** — "A quality criterion is some requirement that defines what the product should be. By thinking about different kinds of criteria, you will be better able to plan tests that discover important problems fast. Each of the items on this list can be thought of as a potential risk area."

**RST, developsense.com/rst-approach** — "Quality is always value to some person who matters."

**RST, satisfice.com/rapid-testing-methodology** — "Testing's purpose is to discover the status of the product and any threats to its value, so that our clients can make informed decisions about it." … Premise 8: "Testers accept responsibility for the quality of their work, although they cannot control the quality of the product."
