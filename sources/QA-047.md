## b. Best primary source

**CT-AI v2.0 §4.1–4.2** — the only authoritative document I found that treats nondeterminism, oracles, and adversarial prompting as *one connected testing problem* rather than as separate security or ML-ops concerns. It maps onto the deliverable almost line by line:

| Deliverable step | Section |
|---|---|
| why run it several times | §4.1.2 *Rationale for a Statistical Approach* |
| "clear criteria" is hard | §4.1.3 *Test Oracles for AI-Based Systems* (five named solutions) |
| normal prompts + edge cases | §4.2.1 *Testing Generative AI* ('input explosion problem') |
| prompt-injection attempts | §4.2.2 *Red Teaming* (5-step process, K3 objective) |
| the exercise itself | HO-4.2.3 *Exploratory Testing of a Large Language Model* |
| recording failures | §2.1.1 quality characteristics + §2.2.1 acceptance-criteria table |

The hinge is §2.1.1's definition of **AI robustness** — correctness maintained "regardless of circumstances, such as the presence of **biased, adversarial, or invalid data inputs**". Those three are exactly the deliverable's three prompt buckets, giving the lesson a spine that comes from the standard rather than from the task author. Two free companions the syllabus does not replace:

1. **OWASP LLM01:2025 Prompt Injection** (https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm01-prompt-injection/) — the injection taxonomy CT-AI omits: direct vs indirect, plus nine attack scenarios.
2. **Hamel Husain, "LLM Evals: Everything You Need to Know"** (https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/) — the eval-construction method, and the sharpest disagreement (§d).

## c. What it leaves out

- **"Hallucination" never appears in §4.2.** Across pp. 41–43 the word is absent; §4.2.1 frames GenAI quality as "correctness, coherence, and creativity". The task's headline failure mode is unnamed in the section that should name it. (Ch. 9 *AI-Specific Terms*, pp. 69–77, is unread — check there before asserting it is absent from the whole document.)
- **Prompt injection gets exactly one clause.** In all of §4.2.2: "such as **indirect prompt injection** attacks and the hiding of malicious content in documents used by RAG." No direct injection, no taxonomy, no example payload. A learner cannot build the injection third of the eval set from this. Hence OWASP.
- **The acceptance-criteria table (§2.2.1) has no GenAI row.** Its worked examples are image recognition (95% accuracy), defect prediction (90% recall), engine management, video streaming, an autonomous drone, an edge AI device — every one classical ML or control software. The learner must invent the LLM criteria themselves, and that invention *is* the hard part of the deliverable.
- **No number for "several".** §4.1.2 requires a suite "sufficiently large to provide statistically significant results" and stops. Nothing on the n=20, run-it-3-times scale a learner actually works at.
- **Temperature named but not used.** §4.2.1 lists temperature and context window as factors affecting output; nothing tells the learner to *pin* them, or to vary them deliberately as a test-design lever. Pinning temperature vs. leaving it free changes what re-running even means.
- **No eval-set artifact** — no file format, no record-keeping schema, no regression story.
- **§5.1.2 "Testing for Bias" (p. 48) is unread** and sits in Ch. 5 *Input Data Testing* — i.e. training-data bias, likely not the output-level bias this task asks about. Verify before relying on it.

## d. Credible disagreement

**1. Husain vs. CT-AI on benchmarks and off-the-shelf metrics — a direct clash.** CT-AI §4.2.1 closes on benchmark suites: "By measuring GenAI against these benchmarks, areas for improvement can be identified in a systematic, reproducible manner." Husain, asked whether to use ready-to-use evaluation metrics: **"No. Generic evaluations waste time and create false confidence."** They "measure abstract qualities that may not matter for your use case. Good scores on them don't mean your system works." His replacement is **error analysis first** — "the most important activity in evals… helps you decide what evals to write in the first place" — 100+ traces, open coding, axial coding, to saturation. For this task Husain is the better guide: the deliverable is a *bespoke* eval set for one feature, precisely the case where benchmarks say nothing.

**2. Binary vs. threshold scoring.** CT-AI §2.2 asserts criteria "often need to be statistical, probabilistic, or threshold-based **rather than binary**". Husain: "Binary evaluations force clearer thinking and more consistent labeling. Likert scales introduce significant challenges: the difference between adjacent points (like 3 vs 4) is subjective and inconsistent across annotators." Both are defensible — ISTQB is picturing a classifier with 10k labelled cases, Husain a hand-graded LLM feature. Teach the *split*: binary verdict per prompt, threshold on the pass *rate* across runs. That satisfies both and is what the deliverable needs.

**3. LLM-as-judge, lightly caveated vs. properly validated.** CT-AI §4.2.1: "a second GenAI system can often be used to automatically determine the test result… Such approaches should be used carefully, as they may reproduce similar biases or errors." Husain supplies the missing mechanism: hand-label ground truth and measure the judge's **True Positive Rate and True Negative Rate on a held-out labelled test set**. "Should be used carefully" is not a method.

**4. RST — narrower than usual here, so don't overplay it.** CT-AI already grants exploratory testing its own hands-on objective (HO-4.2.3), so the standard ISTQB-vs-RST fight is largely absent. What Bach & Bolton add is the *finding* stance: their LLM "syndromes" work (developsense.com, "Voldemort Syndrome", Dec 2024) is a catalogue of failure patterns collected by interactive use since June 2023, and its claim is that **"you don't need an intimate, insider's knowledge of how a technology works to show that there are problems about it."** An eval set only tests failures you already thought of; syndromes come from playing. Run the exploratory session *before* freezing the eval set, and let it supply the edge cases.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

- **HO-4.2.3, adapted verbatim** — the syllabus's own exercise: an exploratory session sheet testing "the LLM's ability to generate test cases using 2-value and 3-value boundary value analysis", debriefed by verifying "the correctness and completeness of the output." Elegant for a QA course: the SUT's task is a QA task, so the learner already owns the oracle.
- **§4.2.2's five-step red team** is the deliverable's skeleton: (1) assemble a diverse team, (2) safe test environment, (3) prompt to find vulnerabilities via open-ended exploration or checklists, (4) analyze failures to understand threats, (5) **"Creating datasets from these threats"** — step 5 *is* "create a small evaluation set."
- **OWASP's nine attack scenarios** as the injection bucket, ready-made: direct, indirect, unintentional, model influence via RAG, code injection, payload splitting, multimodal, adversarial suffix, multilingual/obfuscated. Have the learner write one prompt per scenario and mark which are even applicable to their feature — the inapplicable ones teach scoping.
- **The multi-turn detail is the one most learners miss:** §4.2.2 says the core activity is "interactive prompting, where red teamers engage in multi-turn dialogues (e.g., **15-20 turns**)". A one-shot eval set misses these entirely. State it outright as a limit of the deliverable rather than pretending the artifact covers everything.
- **Nondeterminism demo, free and instant:** §4.1.1 notes large networks vary "due to factors such as floating-point precision limits and variations in hardware execution… particularly when using parallel computation or GPUs" — identical prompt, temperature 0, different answers. Run one prompt 10 times before writing any criteria; the spread motivates "run it several times" better than any explanation.
- **Real defect story — Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149 (14 Feb 2024).** Chatbot told a bereaved customer he could apply for a bereavement fare retroactively, contradicting the airline's own static page; the tribunal rejected the argument that the chatbot was a separate legal entity and awarded **$812.02**. Ties §4.1.3's oracle problem to a real consequence: the oracle existed — the company's own policy page — and nobody diffed the bot against it. **Caveat: search summaries only, not the decision text. Re-read the ruling before it ships.**

## Verbatim quotes

All ISTQB quotes from **CT-AI Syllabus v2.0 GA, 2026/04/17**, page numbers as printed.

**§4.1 intro (p. 39):** "A central challenge in testing, known as the 'test oracle problem', arises when determining whether the output produced by the system under test is correct for a given input." · "clearly defining expected results can be difficult or even impossible."

**§4.1.1 (p. 39):** "In practice, AI-based systems span a continuum from fully deterministic, locked-down systems that produce the same output for a given input, to deliberately non-deterministic, self-learning systems that adapt and evolve their behavior." · "Many GenAI systems, such as LLM-based chatbots, are deployed as locked models at runtime but are updated periodically, placing them between fully locked and fully adaptive systems." · "large neural networks can exhibit non-deterministic behavior due to factors such as floating-point precision limits and variations in hardware execution, particularly when using parallel computation or GPUs."

**§4.1.2 (p. 40):** "AI-based systems are fundamentally probabilistic and therefore often exhibit non-deterministic behavior, meaning the same inputs may not always yield the same output." · "a single test… cannot accurately reflect the model's overall AI functional correctness. To confirm that test results reliably overcome this uncertainty, the test suite must be sufficiently large to provide statistically significant results."

**§4.1.3 (pp. 40–41):** "AI outputs can vary even with identical inputs… defining a strict passed/failed oracle often requires setting thresholds or tolerance ranges." Five stated solutions: "Defining Output Boundaries", "Defining Environmental Boundaries", "Expert Consultation", "Specialized Testing", "Proxy Oracles".

**§4.2.1 (pp. 41–42):** "Testing GenAI involves evaluating the correctness, coherence, and creativity of its outputs." · "A significant challenge in testing a GenAI model is the 'input explosion problem', in which inputs can be highly diverse and complex to control." · "There are also multiple parameters to consider, such as temperature and maximum tokens, which will also affect the output. Additionally, the context window… also affects the generated output." · "a second GenAI system can often be used to automatically determine the test result… Such approaches should be used carefully, as they may reproduce similar biases or errors." · "By measuring GenAI against these benchmarks, areas for improvement can be identified in a systematic, reproducible manner."

**§4.2.2 (pp. 42–43):** "Red Teaming (RT) is a systematic, often black-box form of fault attack that probes an AI-based system to identify harmful capabilities, particularly in its outputs." · "especially critical for GenAI due to the vast range of possible inputs and outputs, which create an extensive 'attack space'." · "such as indirect prompt injection attacks and the hiding of malicious content in documents used by RAG." · "for safety and other evaluations, the aim is often to identify how the system might create harmful outputs under ordinary use, such as generating unsafe medical advice, without any adversarial intent from the user." · "multi-turn dialogues (e.g., 15-20 turns)" · steps: "Assembling a diverse team… Providing access… in a safe test environment… Prompting the system to identify vulnerabilities… Analyzing the identified failures… Creating datasets from these threats to support mitigations and system improvements." · "RT… complements Blue Teaming."

**HO-4.2.3 (p. 43):** "Students will be provided with an exploratory testing session sheet focused on testing the LLM's ability to generate test cases using 2-value and 3-value boundary value analysis. As part of the session debrief, they will verify the correctness and completeness of the output."

**§2.1.1 (p. 22), AI robustness:** "the ability of an AI-based system to maintain its level of AI functional correctness regardless of circumstances, such as the presence of biased, adversarial, or invalid data inputs, external interference, adverse environmental conditions, and operator misuse."

**§2.1.2 (p. 23), Non-determinism:** "Even rigorously tested models can exhibit unexpected behavior due to factors such as random number generation or slight variations in input values."

**§2.2 (p. 23):** "For AI-based systems, acceptance criteria often need to be statistical, probabilistic, or threshold-based rather than binary, which introduces additional testing challenges."

**Learning objectives (p. 38):** AI-4.1.2 (K2) "Explain why a statistical approach is often needed when testing AI-based systems" · AI-4.1.3 (K2) "Explain the challenges and solutions relating to test oracles for AI-based systems" · AI-4.2.1 (K2) "Explain how generative AI can be tested" · AI-4.2.2 (K3) "Implement red teaming for GenAI systems" · HO-4.2.3 (H2) "Apply exploratory testing to an LLM performing boundary value analysis".

**OWASP LLM01:2025 Prompt Injection:** "A Prompt Injection Vulnerability occurs when user prompts alter the LLM's behavior or output in unintended ways." · Direct: "occur when a user's prompt input directly alters the behavior of the model in unintended or unexpected ways." · Indirect: "occur when an LLM accepts input from external sources, such as websites or files."

**Hamel Husain, LLM Evals FAQ:** "Error analysis is the most important activity in evals. Error analysis helps you decide what evals to write in the first place." · On ready-to-use metrics: "No. Generic evaluations waste time and create false confidence." · "Binary evaluations force clearer thinking and more consistent labeling." · Judge validation: aim for "high True Positive Rate (TPR) and True Negative Rate (TNR) with your judge on a held out labeled test set." · "at least 100+ fresh traces each review cycle."

**Bolton & Bach, "Voldemort Syndrome" (developsense.com, Dec 2024):** "you don't need an intimate, insider's knowledge of how a technology works to show that there are problems about it." Collecting LLM "syndromes" since June 2023.
