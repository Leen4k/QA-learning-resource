# QA-016 — Smoke and sanity testing

> Dossier, not lesson content. Every claim below must be re-read at source
> before it ships in a lesson.

- **Verdict:** WRONG-DOCUMENT
- **Named source:** https://roadmap.sh/qa
- **Best primary source:** Steve McConnell, "Daily Build and Smoke Test", IEEE Software 13(4), July 1996 — https://stevemcconnell.com/articles/daily-build-and-smoke-test/
- **Exact sections to read:** McConnell, whole article (~1,800 words) + ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §2.2.3 and §2.3 (pp. 30–31) for the confirmation/regression vocabulary the sanity checklist actually needs
- **Read time:** ~20 min

## a. Does the named source cover this?

No. It is a diagram, not a document.

I pulled the roadmap's own PDF export (https://roadmap.sh/pdfs/roadmaps/qa.pdf) and read
it. It is a single node graph with zero prose. Under **Functional Testing** it lists, as
sibling boxes: `UAT`, `Exploratory Testing`, `Sanity Testing`, `Regression Testing`,
`Smoke Testing`, `Unit Testing`, `Integration Testing`. That is the entire treatment on
the diagram — two labels, side by side, with no text distinguishing them.

Clicking a node opens a short blurb from the roadmap's content repo. The whole Sanity
Testing blurb is four sentences (quoted below) plus one link to a BrowserStack marketing
guide. I looked twice for a `smoke-testing@*.md` file in `src/data/roadmaps/qa/content/`
and found none, so the Smoke Testing node appears to have no text at all. (Caveat: GitHub
403'd the API listing; both checks used the HTML tree page, which may paginate. Re-check
in a browser before repeating this in a lesson.)

Against the DELIVERABLE, the page gives a learner nothing: no criteria for what earns a
slot on a smoke list, no time budget, no rule for scoping to a change, no worked example.
It names the topic. It does not teach it.

**The bigger problem with this task as written.** The `wants` line asks the learner to
"know the difference between a smoke test and a sanity check." The ISTQB Glossary says
there is no difference — `sanity test` is listed as a *synonym* of `smoke test`, alongside
`confidence test` and `intake test`. The smoke/sanity distinction the deliverable assumes
is a folk taxonomy from QA blogs, not something any standards body asserts. The lesson
should teach the two *activities* (which are genuinely different and both worth doing) and
be explicit that the *labels* are contested — not drill a definition split that a learner
will get marked wrong on in an ISTQB exam.

## b. Best primary source

**McConnell, "Daily Build and Smoke Test"** — https://stevemcconnell.com/articles/daily-build-and-smoke-test/
This is the origin document for the practice. Free, stable, ~10 minutes, and it is the only
thing I found that actually tells you how to *size* a smoke test rather than just define it:
end-to-end, not exhaustive, and it must grow as the system grows. That is exactly the
judgment the deliverable requires.

**For the sanity half, ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §2.2.3 + §2.3** — https://istqb.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/ISTQB_CTFL_Syllabus_v4.0.1.pdf
Not because it covers sanity testing (it doesn't — see below) but because it supplies the
correct vocabulary for what a change-focused checklist *is*: confirmation testing plus
scoped regression testing, sized by impact analysis.

**Do not cite the CTFL syllabus as a source on smoke or sanity testing.** I read pp. 24–31
directly. "Smoke" and "sanity" appear in none of §2.1.4 (DevOps), §2.2.2 (Test Types),
§2.2.3 (Confirmation and Regression Testing) or §2.3 (Maintenance Testing) — the four
sections where they would have to live — nor in the Chapter 2 keyword list, nor in any
learning objective FL-2.2.1 through FL-2.3.1. Foundation does not teach this topic.

## c. What it leaves out

McConnell is writing to a 1996 daily-build audience and leaves out most of what this
deliverable needs:

- **No selection criterion.** End-to-end and non-exhaustive, yes — but no rule for which
  specific flows earn a slot. The lesson must supply one; the obvious candidate is risk ×
  traffic: the paths that, if broken, mean "stop the release."
- **Nothing on manual smoke checklists.** His smoke test is automated and CI-triggered. A
  *10-minute human* checklist also needs step granularity, a pass/fail wording convention,
  and an explicit environment/build-identity check (which version am I even looking at?).
- **No treatment of the sanity side at all.** Scoping a check to a change is CTFL §2.3
  territory, and even there you get only the three sizing factors, not a method.
- **No stopping rule.** Neither source says what to do when the smoke checklist fails — who
  it goes to, whether testing continues. McConnell only covers the *build* breaking.

## d. Credible disagreement

Three positions, and they genuinely conflict — do not blend them:

1. **ISTQB:** smoke and sanity are the same thing. `sanity test` is a synonym of
   `smoke test`. There is nothing to distinguish.
2. **Mainstream practitioner blogs** (BrowserStack, Guru99 — and roadmap.sh's own linked
   resource): they are different. Smoke = wide and shallow across the whole build, done
   before testing starts; sanity = narrow and deep on the changed area, done after a fix.
   This is the version most working teams use and the version the deliverable assumes.
   It has no standards backing.
3. **Rapid Software Testing (Bach & Bolton):** the taxonomy argument is the wrong argument.
   Their objection is that a checklist of pass/fail steps is *checking*, not *testing* —
   "Checking is the mechanistic process of verifying propositions about the product,"
   versus testing as "experiencing, exploring, and experimenting." A green 10-minute smoke
   checklist tells you the propositions you thought to write down still hold; it tells you
   nothing about problems nobody anticipated. RST would want the smoke pass to include
   time for a tester to *look around*, not just tick boxes.

Point 3 is the one worth landing in the lesson, because it is a real limitation of the
artifact the learner is about to build, not a vocabulary squabble.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

- **McConnell's Microsoft build discipline.** Concrete and quotable: developers who break
  the build stop all other work until it is fixed, called in at 3 a.m. if necessary. Good
  for making "what happens when the smoke checklist fails" a real question with a real
  answer rather than an afterthought.
- **The smoke-test-growth arc.** McConnell's "as the system grows, the smoke test can grow
  to 30 minutes, an hour, or more" is a ready-made exercise: give the learner a smoke list
  that has bloated past the 10-minute budget and make them cut it. Deciding what to *remove*
  teaches the selection criterion better than writing from blank.
- **Google Testing Blog, "Just Say No to More End-to-End Tests"** (2015-04) —
  https://testing.googleblog.com/2015/04/just-say-no-to-more-end-to-end-tests.html
  The 500-test / 12-hour / 10–15%-failing-at-any-time scenario is the best available
  illustration of why the 10-minute cap is the point of the exercise. Note when adapting:
  it is a constructed scenario, not a documented incident. Do not present it as a case study.
- **Gap:** I did not find a citable real defect story where a smoke check caught (or missed)
  a specific production failure. If the lesson wants one, it will need to come from the
  learner's own workplace or be written as an explicit hypothetical.

## Verbatim quotes

**McConnell, "Daily Build and Smoke Test" (IEEE Software 13(4), July 1996):**
- "a relatively simple check to see whether the product 'smokes' when it runs."
- "It does not have to be exhaustive, but it should be capable of exposing major problems."
- "The smoke test should exercise the entire system from end to end."
- "The smoke test must evolve as the system evolves...as the system grows, the smoke test can grow to 30 minutes, an hour, or more."
- "Insist that developers who have broken the build stop all other work until they've fixed it."

**ISTQB Glossary, "smoke test"** (via mirror istqb-glossary.page — RE-VERIFY at glossary.istqb.org):
- "A test suite that covers the main functionality of a component or system to determine whether it works properly before planned testing begins."
- Synonyms listed: confidence test, **sanity test**, intake test.

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §2.2.3, Confirmation Testing and Regression Testing (p. 30):**
- "Confirmation testing confirms that an original defect has been successfully fixed."
- "However, when time or money is short when fixing defects, confirmation testing might be restricted to simply exercising the test steps that should reproduce the failure caused by the defect and checking that the failure does not occur."
- "Regression testing confirms that no adverse consequences have been caused by a change, including a fix that has already been confirmation tested."
- "It is advisable first to perform an impact analysis to recognize the extent of the regression testing. Impact analysis shows which parts of the software could be affected."

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1 §2.3, Maintenance Testing (p. 31)** — how to size a change-focused checklist:
- "The scope of maintenance testing typically depends on: The degree of risk of the change / The size of the existing system / The size of the change"

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0.1, Chapter 2 Keywords (p. 24)** — evidence of absence, quoted in full:
- "acceptance testing, black-box testing, component integration testing, component testing, confirmation testing, functional testing, integration testing, maintenance testing, non-functional testing, regression testing, shift left, system integration testing, system testing, test level, test object, test type, white-box testing"

**Bach & Bolton, "Testing and Checking Refined"** (satisfice.com/blog/archives/856, orig. 2013-03-26, rev. 2024-08-10):
- "Testing is the process of evaluating a product by learning about it through experiencing, exploring, and experimenting, which includes to some degree: questioning, study, modeling, observation, inference, etc."
- "Checking is the mechanistic process of verifying propositions about the product."
- "Testing encompasses checking (if checking exists at all), whereas checking cannot encompass testing."

**roadmap.sh QA, "Sanity Testing" node — the complete content:**
- "Sanity testing is a kind of Software Testing performed after receiving a software build, with minor changes in code, or functionality, to ascertain that the bugs have been fixed and no further issues are introduced due to these changes. The goal is to determine that the proposed functionality works roughly as expected. If sanity test fails, the build is rejected to save the time and costs involved in a more rigorous testing."
