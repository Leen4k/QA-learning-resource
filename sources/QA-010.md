# QA-010 — Waterfall and the V-model

> Dossier, not lesson content. Every claim below must be re-read at source
> before it ships in a lesson.

- **Verdict:** WRONG-DOCUMENT
- **Named source:** https://roadmap.sh/qa
- **Best primary source:** ISTQB CTFL Syllabus v3.1 (2018) — https://www.gtb.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/ISTQB-CTFL_Syllabus_2018_V3.1.pdf — with Royce 1970 as the companion primary for the waterfall half
- **Exact sections to read:** CTFL **v3.1** §2.1.1 (p. 28) and §2.2–2.2.4 (pp. 30–37); Royce 1970, pp. 328–333 (Figures 2–6, Steps 1–2); CTFL **v4.0** §1.4.4 (p. 20) for traceability
- **Read time:** ~35 min

## a. Does the named source cover this?

No. It is two unlabelled boxes on a diagram. I read the roadmap's own PDF export
(https://roadmap.sh/pdfs/roadmaps/qa.pdf). Under a node called **SDLC Delivery Model** there
are exactly three children: `V Model`, `Waterfall`, and `Agile Model` (which itself has
Kanban / Scrum / XP / SAFe). No prose, no diagram of the V, no phases, no test levels, no
arrows connecting development work to testing.

Worse than QA-016's case: I checked the roadmap's content repo
(`src/data/roadmaps/qa/content/` in kamranahmedse/developer-roadmap) and there is **no
markdown file for `v-model`, `waterfall`, or `sdlc-delivery-model`** — only `agile-model` and
`safe` exist. Clicking those two nodes gives a learner nothing at all, not even the 2–4
sentence blurb other nodes get. (Caveat: GitHub HTML tree page, which can paginate —
re-check in a browser.) Against the DELIVERABLE the page supplies none of the three things
needed: the shape of the V, the names of the levels, what artefact each level produces.

**Also flag for the writing pass:** the plan pairs Waterfall and the V-model as if they were
two flavours of one thing. They are not, and the difference *is* the lesson — in Waterfall
testing is one phase at the end; in the V-model each development phase has a test level
paired with it and test design starts then. CTFL v3.1 §2.1.1 states this contrast
explicitly (quoted below).

## b. Best primary source

**ISTQB CTFL Syllabus v3.1 (2018), §2.1.1 + §2.2.** The only free, stable, testing-specific
document I found that does both halves of the job: §2.1.1 draws the Waterfall/V-model
contrast in two paragraphs, and §2.2.1–2.2.4 then gives, per test level, a **Test basis** (the
work product you test *against*), a **Test object**, typical defects, and responsibilities.
The left arm of the V is §2.2's "test basis" lists; the right arm is its "test objects" —
exactly the trace the deliverable asks for.

**Important: this material was deleted in v4.0.** I read the ASTQB-hosted v4.0 PDF
(https://astqb.org/assets/documents/ISTQB_CTFL_Syllabus-v4.0.pdf, pp. 23–29) end to end.
"V-model" appears **once in the whole of Chapter 2**, as a parenthetical example in the §2.1
preamble. It is not in the Chapter 2 keyword list, and no learning objective FL-2.1.1–2.1.6
mentions it — v4.0 swapped sequential-model coverage for DevOps, shift-left and
retrospectives. Cite v3.1 for the model, and say plainly that current Foundation-level ISTQB
has dropped it.

**Companion primary: Royce, "Managing the Development of Large Software Systems", Proc. IEEE
WESCON, Aug 1970, pp. 328–338** — http://www.cs.umd.edu/class/spring2003/cmsc838p/Process/waterfall.pdf
— the origin document for the waterfall diagram; its Step 2 ("Document the Design") is the
deliverable in miniature. For traceability vocabulary add **CTFL v4.0 §1.4.4** (p. 20).

## c. What it leaves out

- **Neither source draws the V.** CTFL v3.1 §2.1.1 describes it in one paragraph and never
  diagrams it; Royce predates it and never mentions a V. The lesson supplies the picture.
- **No worked trace, and the left arm is only implied.** v3.1 gives per-level lists but never
  says who authors those artefacts or when, and nobody carries a single named requirement
  down the descending side and back up. That synthesis is the lesson's job.
- **"Evidence" is not a term either source uses.** v3.1 says *test basis* and *work product*;
  Royce says *documentation*; v4.0 §1.4.4 says *testware*. Pick one and define it once.
- **No exit criteria.** Neither source says what makes a stage's evidence *sufficient* to proceed — and real V-model projects live or die on that.

## d. Credible disagreement

I did not find a Rapid Software Testing text specifically on the V-model this session and am
not going to invent one. What I did read is stronger anyway — the dissent starts with the
model's own author:

1. **Royce disagrees with Royce.** Immediately after presenting the seven-box waterfall
   (Figure 2) he writes that it "is risky and invites failure," and his Figure 4 caption says
   the design iterations "are never confined to the successive steps." The single-pass
   waterfall taught in textbooks is the thing Royce drew in order to argue against it; his
   actual recommendation was to build the system twice.
2. **Larman & Basili (IEEE Computer, June 2003)** document that the misreading was
   institutionalised. DoD-Std-2167 mandated document-driven single-pass waterfall; a 1999
   review found that of a $37 billion DoD sample, "75% of the projects failed or were never
   used, and only 2% were used without extensive modification." MIL-STD-498 replaced 2167A in
   Dec 1994 with a section titled "Removing the Waterfall Bias."
3. **Parnas & Clements (1986)**, quoted in the same paper, take the position most useful
   here: the waterfall ideal is desirable but unattainable — users cannot articulate what
   they want, details only surface in implementation, external forces invalidate earlier
   decisions. Their conclusion is not "abandon the model" but "fake it": produce the
   documentation as if the process *had* been sequential.

Present the V-model as neither correct nor obsolete, but as a *documentation contract* — it
says what artefact must exist at each stage; the argument is over whether producing them in
that order is possible.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

- **Royce's Figure 6 (p. 333) is the deliverable, pre-built.** Six numbered documents mapped
  onto the phase boxes: No. 1 Software Requirements, No. 2 Preliminary Design, No. 3
  Interface Design, No. 4 Final Design (as built), No. 5 Test Plan **and** Test Results,
  No. 6 Operating Instructions. Hand the learner this figure, have them redraw it as a V,
  ask which of the six are *test* evidence. The sting: "Test Plan" and "Test Results" share
  one document number — planned on the way down, filled in on the way up.
- **The v3.1 test-basis lists are the left arm.** §2.2.1–2.2.4 give them per level — e.g.
  component → "detailed design, code, data model, component specifications"; system →
  "system and software requirement specifications, risk analysis reports, use cases, epics
  and user stories, models of system behavior, state diagrams, system and user manuals".
  Give the learner one requirement plus these four lists and the trace writes itself.
- **Real project stories, both from Larman & Basili:** the 1972 IBM FSD Trident submarine
  command-and-control system (>1M LOC, $100k/day late penalty, four six-month iterations);
  and NASA's shuttle avionics system, 1977–80, 17 iterations over 31 months, whose team wrote
  *apologetically* that "the ideal software development life cycle [the waterfall model]
  could not be strictly applied." The projects most associated with heavyweight process were
  not run single-pass.
- **Gap:** no free, citable worked example of a *single requirement* traced end-to-end through
  a V with named evidence artefacts. The lesson must construct one, on Figure 6's skeleton.

## Verbatim quotes

**ISTQB CTFL v3.1 §2.1.1, Software Development and Software Testing (p. 28)** — the
load-bearing contrast:
- "In the Waterfall model, the development activities (e.g., requirements analysis, design, coding, testing) are completed one after another. In this model, test activities only occur after all other development activities have been completed."
- "Unlike the Waterfall model, the V-model integrates the test process throughout the development process, implementing the principle of early testing. Further, the V-model includes test levels associated with each corresponding development phase, which further supports early testing (see section 2.2 for a discussion of test levels). In this model, the execution of tests associated with each test level proceeds sequentially, but in some cases overlapping occurs."
- Good-testing characteristics, first bullet: "For every development activity, there is a corresponding test activity"

**ISTQB CTFL v3.1 §2.2, Test Levels (p. 30)** — the five attributes that structure the trace,
across the levels "Component testing, Integration testing, System testing, Acceptance testing":
- "Test levels are characterized by the following attributes: Specific objectives / Test basis, referenced to derive test cases / Test object (i.e., what is being tested) / Typical defects and failures / Specific approaches and responsibilities"

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0 §2.1 preamble (p. 24)** — the *entire* v4.0 treatment of the V-model:
- "Examples of SDLC models include: sequential development models (e.g., waterfall model, V-model), iterative development models (e.g., spiral model, prototyping), and incremental development models (e.g., Unified Process)."

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0, Chapter 2 keyword list (p. 23)** — evidence of absence, quoted in full:
- "acceptance testing, black-box testing, component integration testing, component testing, confirmation testing, functional testing, integration testing, maintenance testing, non-functional testing, regression testing, shift-left, system integration testing, system testing, test level, test object, test type, white-box testing"

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0 §1.4.4, Traceability between the Test Basis and Testware (p. 20):**
- "Traceability of test cases to requirements can verify that the requirements are covered by test cases."

**Royce 1970 (pp. 329, 332–333)** — the waterfall's author on the waterfall:
- "I believe in this concept, but the implementation described above is risky and invites failure." (p. 329, immediately under Figure 2)
- Figure 3 caption: "Hopefully, the iterative interaction between the various phases is confined to successive steps." / Figure 4 caption: "Unfortunately, for the process illustrated, the design iterations are never confined to the successive steps."
- "The testing phase which occurs at the end of the development cycle is the first event for which timing, storage, input/output transfers, etc., are experienced as distinguished from analyzed... In effect the development process has returned to the origin and one can expect up to a 100-percent overrun in schedule and/or costs." (p. 329)
- "The first rule of managing software development is ruthless enforcement of documentation requirements... An acceptable written description forces the designer to take an unequivocal position and provide tangible evidence of completion. It prevents the designer from hiding behind the — 'I am 90-percent finished' — syndrome month after month." (p. 332)
- Figure 6 caption: "Step 2: Insure that documentation is current and complete — at least six uniquely different documents are required." (p. 333)

**Larman & Basili, "Iterative and Incremental Development: A Brief History", IEEE Computer 36(6), June 2003, pp. 47–56:**
- "Many—incorrectly—view Royce's paper as the paragon of single-pass waterfall. In reality, he recommended an approach somewhat different than what has devolved into today's waterfall concept". (p. 48)
- Walker Royce on his father: "He was always a proponent of iterative, incremental, evolutionary development. His paper described the waterfall as the simplest description, but that it would not work for all but the most straightforward projects." (p. 48)
- "Of a total $37 billion for the sample set, 75% of the projects failed or were never used, and only 2% were used without extensive modification." (p. 52)
- Brooks, ICSE 1995 keynote: "The waterfall model is wrong!" (p. 52); MIL-STD-498, Dec 1994, per Maj. George Newberry's summary section "Removing the Waterfall Bias" (p. 53)
