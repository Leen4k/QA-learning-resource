## b. Best primary source

Two sections do the work, and they are deliberately cross-linked:

- **§5.1.5 Test Case Prioritization (p.49)** — the three strategies: risk-based,

- **§5.2 Risk Management (p.50)** — defines risk-based testing.
- **§5.2.1 Risk Definition and Risk Attributes (p.50)** — the deliverable's two

- **§5.2.2 Project vs Product Risks (p.51)** — which risks a *tester* can act on;
  keeps the fifteen rows out of schedule-complaint territory.
- **§5.2.3 Product Risk Analysis (pp.51–52)** — identification vs assessment,
  quantitative (multiply) vs qualitative (matrix), six uses of the result.
- **§5.2.4 Product Risk Control (p.52)** — what you *do* once ranked.

Learning objectives (p.46) nearly match the deliverable's level: FL-5.1.5 is **(K3)
Apply test case prioritization**, FL-5.2.1 only **(K1) Identify risk level by using
risk likelihood and risk impact**. Note the asymmetry — ISTQB asks you to *apply*
prioritization but merely to *identify* risk level.

## c. What it leaves out

1. **The matrix itself.** §5.2.3 says "In the qualitative approach the risk level
   can be determined using a risk matrix" — and never shows one. No axes, no
   3×3-vs-5×5, no cell labels, no thresholds, no example. **The deliverable is
   precisely the artifact the syllabus declines to draw.**
2. **Scale definitions.** §5.2.1 defines likelihood as a real probability
   ("greater than zero and less than one"); qualitative practice uses
   Low/Medium/High. The syllabus never bridges those — never says what "High
   likelihood" means in percent, weeks, or users affected.
3. **Where fifteen ideas come from.** §5.2.3 offers only "brainstorming,
   workshops, interviews, or cause-effect diagrams" — no checklist, no prompt
   list. A learner at a blank table gets no help. (HTSM fills this — §e.)
4. **The cut line.** §5.1.5 orders tests; §5.2.3 says to minimize residual risk.
   Neither says "you have three hours, run rows 1–6, and here is how to say so out
   loud." The stated want — *when time is limited* — is unanswered.
5. **Any caveat on the arithmetic.** §5.2.3 says risk level "is calculated as the
   multiplication of risk likelihood and risk impact", with no warning that
   multiplying ordinal labels is unsound (§d).
6. **Re-ranking.** The table reads as an up-front artifact; only §5.3 mentions
   "Reprioritizing tests when an identified risk becomes an issue", and as a
   control directive rather than a habit.

## d. Credible disagreement

**Two disagreements, pointing in opposite directions. Do not average them.**

**(i) Rapid Software Testing — the grid is overhead.** HTSM v6.0 (Bach, 2024, read
this session) gives Risk Testing as five plain questions — "What kinds of problems
could the product have? Which kinds matter most? Focus on those" — with no axes, no
scores, no table. Risk under RST is a *story* about a possible problem and who it
hurts: assessed by judgement and argued for, not computed.
*Unverified lead:* Bach's 1999 "Heuristic Risk-Based Software Testing" (inside-out
vs outside-in analysis) is the canonical statement. **I could not open it this
session** — the satisfice.com download page serves no text and both mirrors refused
the connection. Retrieve it before quoting; do not repeat the
inside-out/outside-in framing from search-result summaries.

**(ii) Krisper 2021 — the grid can invert the answer.** "Problems with Risk Matrices
Using Ordinal Scales", Michael Krisper, Graz University of Technology —
https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.05440 (free, read this session). The sharper objection,
attacking the deliverable's own format on mathematical grounds: multiplying ordinal
ratings is undefined, and the ranking can put the *smaller* real risk above the
larger one (worked case in §e). Krisper's fix is *more* quantification — real units,
real ranges — the opposite of RST's fix.

**Honest framing:** the table is a forcing function for an argument, not a
calculation. Rank rows by stated reason, using impact and likelihood as the two
questions that generate it; never claim a cell scoring 9 outranks one scoring 8.
Say which of the three positions the lesson teaches, and that the other two exist.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

1. **ISTQB official Sample Exam SET B, Question 32 — FL-5.1.5, K3, 1.0 point**
   (p.29/39) — https://isqi.org/media/5d/9d/2c/1734010921/ISTQB_CTFL40_Sample-Exam-Questions_SET-B_v1.3.2_GTB-edition_engl_EN_.pdf
   (read this session). Six test cases with priorities 1–3 and a dependency chain;
   choose the valid risk-based execution sequence — the only exercise I found that
   drills prioritization at *apply* level. **No answer key in this PDF** (separate
   ISTQB document, not retrieved); my derivation is T1→T2→T3→T5→T4→T6 (option d),
   so check the official key. Also telling: SET B has **no** FL-5.2.1 question,
   jumping Q33 (5.1.7) → Q34 (5.2.4). Even ISTQB doesn't examine the matrix.
2. **HTSM v6.0 Quality Criteria Categories (p.5)** — https://www.developsense.com/resource/htsm.pdf
   (read this session). Ten categories — Capability, Reliability, Usability,
   Charisma, Security, Scalability, Compatibility, Performance, Installability,
   Development — with ~30 sub-criteria, and "Each of the items on this list can be
   thought of as a potential risk area." **The fifteen-idea generator the
   deliverable needs**; stops the list being fifteen happy paths.
3. **Cloudflare global outage, 2 July 2019** — https://blog.cloudflare.com/details-of-the-cloudflare-outage-on-july-2-2019/
   (fetched this session via a summarizing fetch — re-read for exact wording). A WAF
   regex rule pushed worldwide because "The SOP for a rule change specifically
   allows it to be pushed globally"; 80% of traffic lost, 27 minutes; the WAF test
   suite did not "test for runaway CPU utilization." A change *classified low-risk
   to justify skipping staged rollout*, plus a suite ranking functional correctness
   above resource exhaustion — the story for "the row you left off the table."
4. **Krisper §II.A, the MIL-STD-882C oil-leak pair** — one event leaks 1 oz five
   times an hour (52.5 lb/week), the other 10 lb once a week; the grid rates the
   first MEDIUM and the second HIGH. Sixty seconds to run, and it inoculates the
   learner against trusting their own cell colours.
5. **§5.1.5's dependency caveat** (verbatim below) — engineer it in by making one
   of the fifteen rows a low-value prerequisite that has to run first anyway.

## Verbatim quotes

**§5.1.5, p.49** — "Once the test cases and test procedures are specified and assembled into test suites, these test suites can be arranged in a test execution schedule that defines the order in which they are to be run. When prioritizing test cases, different factors can be taken into account. The most commonly used test case prioritization strategies are as follows: • Risk-based prioritization, where the order of test execution is based on the results of risk analysis (see section 5.2.3). Test cases covering the most important risks are executed first. • Coverage-based prioritization... • Requirements-based prioritization, where the order of test execution is based on the priorities of the requirements traced back to the corresponding test cases. Requirement priorities are defined by stakeholders." ... "However, this practice may not work if the test cases or the features being tested have dependencies. If a test case with a higher priority is dependent on a test case with a lower priority, the lower priority test case must be executed first." ... "The order of test execution must also take into account the availability of resources."

**§5.2, p.50** — "The test approach, in which test activities are selected, prioritized, and managed based on risk analysis and risk control, is called risk-based testing."

**§5.2.1, p.50** — "Risk is a potential event, hazard, threat, or situation whose occurrence causes an adverse effect. A risk can be characterized by two factors: • Risk likelihood – the probability of the risk occurrence (greater than zero and less than one) • Risk impact (harm) – the consequences of this occurrence" ... "These two factors express the risk level, which is a measure for the risk. The higher the risk level, the more important is its treatment."

**§5.2.3, p.51** — "Risk assessment can use a quantitative or qualitative approach, or a mix of them. In the quantitative approach the risk level is calculated as the multiplication of risk likelihood and risk impact. In the qualitative approach the risk level can be determined using a risk matrix."

**§5.2.3, pp.51–52** — "Product risk analysis may influence the thoroughness and scope of testing. Its results are used to: • Determine the scope of testing to be carried out • Determine the particular test levels and propose test types to be performed • Determine the test techniques to be employed and the coverage to be achieved • Estimate the test effort required for each task • Prioritize testing in an attempt to find the critical defects as early as possible • Determine whether any activities in addition to testing could be employed to reduce risk"

**p.46, learning objectives** — "FL-5.1.5 (K3) Apply test case prioritization" / "FL-5.2.1 (K1) Identify risk level by using risk likelihood and risk impact" / "FL-5.2.3 (K2) Explain how product risk analysis may influence thoroughness and scope of testing"

**HTSM v6.0, p.2, Risk Testing** — "Imagine a problem, then look for it. 1. What kinds of problems could the product have? 2. Which kinds matter most? Focus on those. 3. How would you detect them if they were there? 4. Make a list of interesting problems and design tests specifically to reveal them. 5. It may help to consult experts, design documentation, past bug reports, or apply risk heuristics."

**HTSM v6.0, p.5 (Quality Criteria Categories)** — "By thinking about different kinds of criteria, you will be better able to plan tests that discover important problems fast. Each of the items on this list can be thought of as a potential risk area."

**Krisper 2021, abstract** — "Among those 24 discussed problems are ordinal scales, semi-quantitative arithmetics, range compression, risk inversion, ambiguity, and neglection of uncertainty. Finally, we make a case for avoiding risk matrices altogether and instead propose using fully quantitative risk assessment methods."

**Krisper 2021, §III.B** — "this ordinal scale is multiplied with other influence factors to get a resulting risk priority number (RPN), although multiplication is not defined on ordinal scales."

**Krisper 2021, §II.A (MIL-STD-882C oil-leak example)** — "This example shows cases where the qualitative risk score does not reflect the actual quantitative risk. Even worse, it results in an inverse order for the events' priorities, which is the consequence of risk inversion."
