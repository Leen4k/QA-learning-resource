# QA-002 — Test oracles: deciding what "correct" means when the spec is silent

> Dossier, not lesson content. Every claim below must be re-read at source
> before it ships in a lesson.

- **Verdict:** WRONG-DOCUMENT
- **Named source:** https://www.istqb.org/certifications/certified-tester-foundation-level
- **Best primary source:** Michael Bolton, *Oracles* (Test Framing, 2010-10-25, 3pp) — https://www.developsense.com/resource/Oracles.pdf — with Barr, Harman, McMinn, Shahbaz & Yoo, *The Oracle Problem in Software Testing: A Survey* (IEEE TSE 41(5), CC BY 3.0) — https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1471263/1/06963470.pdf as the authority behind it
- **Exact sections to read:** Bolton *Oracles* pp.1–3 (all of it) · Barr et al. §1, §2.1–2.4 (Def. 2.4), §5.5, §6, §7
- **Read time:** ~50 min

## a. Does the named source cover this?

No, and it fails twice over.

**The page itself.** Fetched this session through a text proxy: its entire heading
list is *Overview, Audience, Content, Exam Structure, Business Outcomes, More
Information, Download Materials*. Zero instructional text about oracles,
expected results, or judging correctness. It tells you the exam is 40 questions
in 60 minutes with a pass mark of 26. (Same caveat as QA-006: the proxy surfaced
CTFL-AT text, so the named URL looks like a legacy path — verdict unchanged.)

**The syllabus it sells doesn't cover this either.** This is the finding that
matters. I read the CTFL v4.0 index (pp.72–74) directly: there is **no entry for
"oracle", "test oracle", or "expected result"** anywhere in it. The ISTQB
glossary confirms the scope — it defines *test oracle* as "A source to determine
an expected result" and tags the term to Advanced Test Analyst, Advanced Test
Automation, Agile Tester, and six Specialist syllabi. **Foundation Level v4.0 is
not on that list.**

The syllabus uses expected results twice, both presupposing them: §1.4.1 p.19
("Actual test results are compared with the expected results") and §5.5 p.56, a
defect-report field ("Expected results and actual results"). Where they come
from, and what to do when the test basis is silent, is never addressed. Pointing
this task at ISTQB CTFL is a category error, not just a lazy link.

## b. Best primary source

**Bolton, *Oracles*** — a free, stable, self-contained 3-page PDF from
DevelopSense's resource directory (not a blog post). Read this session in full.
It defines an oracle, argues why oracles are necessarily fallible, gives the nine
consistency guidewords with a paragraph of explanation each, and closes with
three worked micro-examples of oracles applied prospectively, in the moment, and
retrospectively. **It is the only source I found that gets a learner to the
deliverable**: the guideword list is a generator — one expected result per
guideword yields ten, and the guideword you used *is* the assumption you made.
(HICCUPPS(F) here; the later mnemonic is FEW HICCUPPS —
https://developsense.com/blog/2012/07/few-hiccupps adds Familiar,
Explainability, World.)

**Barr et al.** is the authoritative document on the topic and supplies what
Bolton assumes: the formal definition (Def. 2.4), the four-way taxonomy
(specified §4, derived §5, implicit §6, no automatable oracle §7), and — most
relevant here — §5.5 and §7 on why informal documentation cannot serve as a
complete oracle. Use it for framing, not method; it is written for automation
researchers.

## c. What it leaves out

Of Bolton's *Oracles*:

1. **How to write an expected result down.** The paper is about *recognizing a
   problem when you meet it*, not pre-stating a result. The deliverable asks for
   ten written expected results; Bolton gives you nothing on form or notation.
2. **Assumptions as a recorded artifact.** He says oracles conflict and "the
   tester's role is not to decide the matter, but to make people aware of the
   disagreement" — but never says *write the assumption next to the expectation*.
   That half of the deliverable has no source; it is the lesson's own move.
3. **Coverage of a single flow.** Guidewords, not a checklist — nothing on
   timing, persistence, concurrency, or error states as expectation categories.
4. **Any full worked example.** Three one-liners, no end-to-end flow.
5. **When ten is enough.** No stopping heuristic — he cites Weinberg: "heuristic
   devices don't tell you when to stop." Its onward reference to Kaner's BBST at
   testingeducation.org 404'd this session; don't send a learner there.

Of Barr et al.: no method, no exercise, and the human tester is framed as a *cost*
to be minimized (§7, "Human Oracle Cost"), not a skill to be built.

## d. Credible disagreement

**ISTQB and RST disagree about whether an expected result can exist in advance.**

ISTQB's glossary makes the oracle *a source* — singular, external, consulted to
*determine* the expected result, after which §1.4.1's comparison is mechanical.
Bolton's definition is a different object: "a heuristic principle or mechanism by
which someone recognizes a problem" — plural, fallible, and often applied only
*after* the observation. His flat contradiction: "we often do not have oracles
that establish a definite correct or incorrect result in advance," and "there can
be no absolute oracle."

The sharp edge for this task: in RST terms, "write ten expected results" is
writing ten **checks**, not ten tests. Say that to the learner rather than hiding
it — and note that it makes the *assumptions* column the more valuable half of
their own deliverable, because that column is where the oracle is named.

Second disagreement, on the premise of the task itself. The plan frames
incomplete requirements as an obstacle to work around. Bolton treats the
incompleteness as a finding: "Insufficient clarity about requirements is both a
test result and a project risk," and insufficient requirements are "precisely a
problem that testing can help to solve"
(https://developsense.com/blog/2013/11/very-short-blog-posts-9-insufficient-requirements).
Under that reading, the ten assumptions the learner writes down are the
deliverable's actual output — a list of questions for whoever owns the feature.

## e. Worked examples worth adapting

- **Bolton, *Oracles* p.3 — three modes, three one-liners.** Design-time ("If the
  data doesn't get transmitted to the server after I press update, that would be
  inconsistent with an implicit purpose and an explicit claim"); in-the-moment
  ("Hey… that account balance is *negative*!"); retrospective (recognizing
  non-standard behaviour weeks later). Best available structure for the lesson.
- **Bolton p.1 — "That's not a bug; that's a feature."** Tester and program
  manager applying *different* oracles, "Neither is wrong." That is the
  assumptions column dramatized in two sentences.
- **Kaner, "The Oracle Problem and the Teaching of Software Testing"
  (2012-09-11)** — https://kaner.com/?p=190 — supplies the failure modes Bolton
  omits: **the miss** ("you believe the program has passed even though it did
  something wrong") and **the false alarm** ("you believe the program has failed
  even though it has behaved appropriately"). Plus a ready-made exercise: test a
  new OpenOffice Calc using Excel as the comparable-product oracle. Kaner insists
  students name *specific* oracles, not the heuristic list — use that to grade.
- **Barr et al. §6, p.518 — implicit oracles.** "buffer overflows and segfaults
  are nearly always errors": expectations you hold with no spec at all. Paired
  with Ricca & Tonella's finding that "60 percent of the web applications
  exhibited anomalies and execution failures" — you can find real bugs before
  writing a single requirement down.
- **Barr et al. §6 counterweight** — "Even crashing *may* be considered
  acceptable, or even desired behaviour." Even implicit oracles are contextual.

## Verbatim quotes

**Bolton, *Oracles*, p.1** — "An oracle is a heuristic principle or mechanism by which someone recognizes a problem." … "oracles are fallible and context-dependent. Oracles do not tell us conclusively that there is a problem; rather, they suggest that there *may be* a problem." … "there can be no absolute oracle."

**Bolton, *Oracles*, p.3** — "We usually have a large number of oracles at our disposal before we start testing. Yet we often do not have oracles that establish a definite correct or incorrect result in advance."

**Bolton, *Oracles*, pp.1–2 (HICCUPPS(F))** — History, Image, Comparable Products, Claims, Users' Expectations, Product, Purpose, Standards and Statutes, + Familiarity ("We expect the system to be *inconsistent* with any patterns of familiar problems").

**Barr et al., Abstract, p.507** — "Given an input for a system, the challenge of distinguishing the corresponding desired, correct behavior from potentially incorrect behavior is called the 'test oracle problem'." … "the final source of test oracle information remains the human, who may be aware of informal specifications, expectations, norms and domain specific information that provide informal oracle guidance."

**Barr et al., §5.5, p.518** — textual requirements "are usually informal, intended for other humans, not to support formal logical or mathematical reasoning. Thus, they are often partial and ambiguous, in contrast to specification languages."

**Barr et al., §6, p.518** — "An *implicit test oracle* is one that relies on general, implicit knowledge to distinguish between a system's correct and incorrect behaviour."

**Barr et al., §7, p.519** — "In many cases, however, no such artefact exists so a human tester must verify whether software behaviour is correct given some stimuli."

**ISTQB CTFL v4.0 §1.4.1, p.19** — "Actual test results are compared with the expected results." *(the syllabus's entire treatment)*

**ISTQB glossary** — test oracle: "A source to determine an expected result." Tagged to Advanced and Specialist syllabi only; **not** Foundation v4.0.
